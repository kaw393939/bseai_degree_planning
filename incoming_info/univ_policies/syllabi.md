Best Practices in Syllabus Design

What is the purpose of the syllabus? The syllabus is an essential course document
that provides students with their first insight into who you are and what they will be
expected to do in the course. In many ways, this document sets the tone for the course
and establishes the expectations for both instructor responsibility and student
responsibility. As such, the syllabus is often thought of as a course contract. It should
clearly outline course learning outcomes, how those outcomes will be assessed, how
students will be graded, and all other course policies, requirements, and expectations.
By reading the syllabus, students should be able to understand what it will take to be
successful in the course, as well as know what is expected of them and when.

● Expectations for Class Etiquette
○ Set criteria for how you expect students to behave in class.
○ E.g., “Students are expected to follow NJIT’s Code of Student Conduct.
Students should be polite, active participants and respect their instructor,
classmates, as well as ideas or opinions that differ from their own. While

attacks.”
● Weekly Expectations
○ Include a clear explanation of course expectations and what students
need to do each week to be successful.
■ Example for face-to-face classes: “Each week, students will attend
two lectures in class, and they will be expected to complete reading
and homework assignments between each class session.”
■ Example for online classes: “This course is organized by weekly
modules. Each week, students must watch a lecture video,
complete a reading assignment, and participate in a class
discussion forum by Friday at 11:59pm.”

“Backward Planning” and Engaged-Learning Syllabi
It may be helpful to think of the syllabus as a course planning document. In addition to
providing students with clear expectations and requirements for how to be successful in
the course, the syllabus should help you map out what you need students to do in order
to achieve the learning outcomes, as well as the best ways for you to assess those
achievements. It serves as a foundation for the course as you plan out lessons,
activities, and assessments, and is a critical component in the course design process.
One strategy is to employ “backward planning,” which means starting with the desired
course outcomes and then moving backward to the teaching techniques, texts, and
course assignments that will best enable students to achieve those goals. Rather than
starting with what you want them to KNOW, start with what you want them to be able to
DO with what they know.
As such, to design for optimal student engagement and student-centered learning, the
syllabus should reflect assignment-centered rather than content-centered learning.
Engaged-learning courses provide opportunities for students to reflect on their own
learning (metacognition), challenge misconceptions they may have brought to class,
make connections between course material and their own experiences, or apply course
material to specific problems.
