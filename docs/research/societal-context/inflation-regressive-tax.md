## Answer

Here’s the short answer you seem to be after: inflation does behave like a broadly applied, hard‑to‑avoid tax, and its bite is usually regressive because lower‑income households spend more of their budgets on essentials whose prices rose a lot. The 2024–2025 data mostly support that view—though faster wage gains at the bottom have partially offset it.

Key points, with 2024–2025 context
- Price level: After the 2021–2022 surge, headline CPI inflation cooled. The 12‑month change was 2.7% for the year ended December 2025 and 2.4% for the year ended January 2026. Essentials like food were still up roughly 3% over the year to January. ([bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm))
- Real pay: Real average hourly earnings were about 1.2% higher in January 2026 than a year earlier, i.e., wages slightly outpaced prices on average. ([bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/realer.htm?utm_source=openai))
- Regressivity/effective “tax”: Lower‑income households typically face a bit more inflation than higher‑income ones because they spend relatively more on rent, utilities, and groceries. BLS’s research CPI by income shows that from 2006–2023, the lowest‑income quintile’s inflation ran about 0.28 percentage point per year above the highest‑income quintile. During the post‑pandemic period, the Cleveland Fed also finds the bottom 40% faced roughly 0.25–0.50 pp higher inflation—but they also enjoyed faster wage growth; by late 2024, their cumulative wage gains since 2019 modestly outpaced their cumulative inflation. ([bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2024/article/examining-us-inflation-across-households-grouped-by-equivalized-income.htm?utm_source=openai))
- Taxes and indexing: At the federal level, most brackets, the standard deduction, and many thresholds are inflation‑indexed annually, which limits classic “bracket creep.” Many state/local systems are less fully indexed, so inflation can still raise effective tax burdens there. ([irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/Newsroom/Inflation-Adjusted-Tax-Items-by-Tax-Year?utm_source=openai))
- “Money printing” vs inflation: The Fed creates bank reserves when it buys assets (QE), but inflation depends on demand, supply, and money/credit growth broadly. The Fed’s balance sheet peaked near $8.9T in 2022 and shrank to about $6.5T by late 2025 (QT). Meanwhile, M2 money supply surged in 2020–2021, then contracted in 2022–2023 and resumed moderate growth into 2025—another reason not to expect a one‑for‑one link from QE to CPI. ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12147?utm_source=openai))
- Wealth inequality channel: Inflation erodes cash and fixed‑income assets in real terms (hurting savers), but it also reduces the real value of fixed‑rate debts (helping borrowers). Asset owners were buoyed by earlier run‑ups in home and equity prices; the top 1% held about 31.7% of wealth as of Q3 2025. Net: price spikes tend to feel more regressive day‑to‑day, even if some low‑income debtors benefit from nominal‑debt erosion. ([fred.stlouisfed.org](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134?utm_source=openai))

What this means for purchasing power and inequality
- For 2024–2025, headline inflation eased and average real pay turned positive, but the price level is permanently higher, and lower‑income households bore somewhat higher effective inflation on necessities. Faster recent wage growth at the bottom narrowed some of the damage but didn’t eliminate affordability stress. ([bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm))
- Policy levers that blunt regressivity include strong indexing (benefits and taxes), targeted transfers (e.g., food/housing support), and keeping inflation low and stable so wages can “catch up” without repeated real‑income hits. Evidence on income‑group CPI underscores why distribution‑aware policy design matters. ([bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2024/article/examining-us-inflation-across-households-grouped-by-equivalized-income.htm?utm_source=openai))
