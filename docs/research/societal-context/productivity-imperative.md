## Answer

Short answer: Faster productivity growth is the cleanest, least-painful way to shrink the debt-to-GDP ratio over time, but it is not the only way—and relying on growth alone is risky. Durable solutions pair pro-productivity policies with a credible path to primary budget surpluses.

Debt arithmetic (why growth matters)
- Change in debt/GDP ≈ (interest rate − GDP growth) × current debt/GDP − primary surplus.
- You can lower the debt ratio by: (a) raising growth (especially productivity), (b) running primary surpluses (more revenue or less non‑interest spending), (c) inflating/financially repressing it away, or (d) restructuring/default (not relevant for the U.S.). Historically, the best outcomes mix (a) and (b).

What economic history suggests
- Post‑WWII U.S. debt fell sharply as rapid growth and moderate inflation outpaced interest costs, aided by primary surpluses.
- In the 1990s, strong productivity gains plus consolidation lowered the debt ratio.
- Lesson: growth tailwinds make fiscal repair easier; consolidation without growth is politically hard and economically costly.

A practical playbook: raise productivity and safeguard the fiscal path
1) Human capital (highest long‑run returns)
- Early childhood: targeted pre‑K and intensive tutoring to recover learning losses.
- K‑12: evidence‑based curriculum, teacher quality, high‑dosage tutoring, career/technical tracks.
- Post‑secondary: completion-focused funding, apprenticeships, short-cycle credentials tied to labor-market data.
- Immigration: expand high‑skill and STEM visas; clear backlogs; retain U.S.-trained grads.
- Labor force participation: expand EITC/WOTC, childcare access, reform occupational licensing, targeted reentry programs.

2) Innovation and diffusion
- Increase mission‑driven public R&D (energy, biotech, AI, advanced manufacturing) and strengthen tech transfer.
- Support competitive research clusters; modernize patent quality; ease firm formation and scale-up financing.
- Promote technology diffusion to SMEs via extension services and digital adoption grants.

3) Capital deepening and infrastructure
- Accelerate electricity transmission, grid modernization, port/airport upgrades, broadband; streamline project delivery and permitting.
- Housing supply reform (zoning, by-right infill, permitting speed) so workers can move to productive regions.
- Congestion pricing and logistics digitization to cut time costs.

4) Market dynamism and trade
- Competition policy that encourages entry and reduces barriers (especially in services).
- Sensible exposure to global markets and allied supply chains to widen markets for U.S. innovators.

5) Tax policy that tilts toward growth
- Make cost recovery neutral (full expensing for new investment, including intangibles); simplify and broaden the base rather than raise statutory rates.
- Shift part of the tax mix toward broad consumption bases (e.g., VAT or carbon tax with border adjustment), using proceeds for deficit reduction and pro‑growth credits.
- Expand EITC and phase‑out cliffs carefully to boost labor supply.

6) Health cost and entitlement sustainability
- Bend the medical cost curve (site‑neutral payments, generic/biosimilar uptake, competitive procurement, transparency).
- Gradual, predictable entitlement adjustments (e.g., index retirement age to longevity, progressive benefit tweaks) to stabilize long‑run primary balances without cutting productive investment.

7) Budget rules and sequencing
- Adopt a medium‑term fiscal anchor (e.g., stabilize debt/GDP on a 5–7 year horizon) with PAYGO and investment protection.
- Phase consolidation gradually: target raising the primary balance by ~1–2% of GDP over several years while protecting high‑return investments above.

Why “growth only” is not enough
- If interest rates exceed growth for long stretches, debt dynamics worsen even with decent growth.
- Growth is uncertain and arrives with lags; a modest, credible primary surplus is the safety belt.
- Inflation surprises can temporarily help but risk higher rates and credibility loss later.

Back‑of‑the‑envelope path
- Combine: +0.5–1.0 percentage point to long‑run productivity growth from the reforms above, plus a phased primary improvement of ~1–2% of GDP, and keep project delivery fast so public investment actually materializes. That mix historically has been sufficient to put debt/GDP on a downward or at least stable path without recessionary “austerity.”
