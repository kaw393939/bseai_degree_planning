## Course Description

Students build a hybrid knowledge system integrating relational databases (SQL), graph databases (Neo4j), and vector embeddings — the three pillars of modern enterprise AI data architecture. But the deeper curriculum is about the relationship between data and knowledge: every schema embodies assumptions, every query frames a question, and every retrieval system can return confident wrong answers.

The defining question: **"What does this data not tell us?"**

Students work with real datasets and build systems that retrieve, relate, and reason over information. Along the way, they confront the limitations of every data structure they use — because in the age of AI, the most dangerous thing is a system that sounds certain about things it doesn't actually know.

## The Stories Behind the Technology

The ways we store and retrieve data were invented by people who saw that the current approach was broken:

- **Edgar F. Codd** was a mathematician at IBM who published "A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks" in 1970. At the time, databases were navigational — you had to know the physical structure of the data to query it. Codd's insight was revolutionary: separate the logical structure (what the data means) from the physical structure (how it's stored). Let users ask *what* they want without specifying *how* to get it. IBM initially resisted the idea (it threatened their existing products), but Codd's relational model won because it was mathematically rigorous and practically powerful. Every time you write `SELECT * FROM users WHERE city = 'Newark'`, you're using Codd's invention.
- **Leonhard Euler** solved the Bridges of Konigsberg problem in 1736 by inventing graph theory — the mathematical foundation for every network, social graph, and knowledge graph you'll ever use. The problem: Can you walk across all seven bridges of Konigsberg exactly once? Euler proved you can't, and in doing so created a new branch of mathematics. When you model entities and relationships in Neo4j, you're standing on Euler's shoulders.
- **Larry Ellison** co-founded Oracle in 1977 because he read Codd's papers and realized nobody was building a commercial product from them. He built the first commercial SQL database and became one of the richest people on Earth. The lesson: sometimes the breakthrough is not the invention (Codd's) but the product (Oracle's). This is relevant to the entrepreneurial mindset: ideas need champions who can build and sell.
- **The vector embedding revolution**: In 2013, Tomas Mikolov at Google published Word2Vec — a way to represent words as vectors in high-dimensional space where similar words are close together. This idea (words as positions in semantic space) underlies every modern AI retrieval system. When your knowledge system searches by meaning rather than keywords, it's using descendants of Mikolov's approach.

## Foundations: The Mathematics of Data

IS 331 is where math becomes directly, visibly useful. Every data technology in this course has mathematical roots:

- **Set theory and relational algebra**: SQL is applied set theory. A table is a set of tuples. A JOIN is a set intersection (or product). A WHERE clause is a predicate filter. Understanding set operations means understanding what SQL can and cannot express — and why some queries are elegant while others are tortured.
- **Graph theory basics**: Nodes and edges, paths and cycles, degree and connectivity. Graph traversal algorithms (BFS, DFS) and why they matter for knowledge graph queries. The shortest path between two entities in your graph has both mathematical and business meaning.
- **Vector spaces and similarity**: What does it mean for two documents to be "similar"? Cosine similarity, Euclidean distance, dot product. Students don't need to build embeddings from scratch, but they need to understand what the numbers mean and when similarity is misleading.
- **Normalization and its tradeoffs**: The first three normal forms are not arbitrary rules — they're mathematical guarantees about data integrity. But normalization has costs (JOIN performance), and sometimes denormalization is the right engineering choice. This is where math meets judgment.
- **Boolean logic for queries**: Complex SQL WHERE clauses, graph pattern matching, and filter pipelines all require fluent boolean reasoning. Students practice translating English questions into logical expressions.

## Security: Protecting Data at Every Layer

Data systems are high-value targets. IS 331 integrates security throughout:

- **SQL injection**: The most famous database attack. Students learn how it works (by building a vulnerable app, then fixing it), why parameterized queries prevent it, and what Bobby Tables means.
- **Access control**: Row-level security, role-based permissions, the principle of least privilege. Who should be able to read this data? Modify it? Delete it? Export it?
- **Data privacy**: PII (personally identifiable information), data minimization, the right to be forgotten. When your knowledge system contains personal data, what are your obligations?
- **Encryption at rest and in transit**: What it means, when it matters, and what it doesn't protect against (an authorized user with bad intentions).
- **AI-specific data risks**: Training data poisoning, embedding bias (vectors inherit the biases of their training data), retrieval of sensitive information through semantic similarity.

## Why Epistemic Humility?

AI can query any database, traverse any graph, and search any vector space. It cannot know that the data is wrong, incomplete, biased, or outdated — and it cannot decide what to do about that uncertainty.

Epistemic Humility is the discipline of knowing the limits of what you know. In data systems, this means understanding that: every schema embodies assumptions about reality; every missing column is invisible data; every aggregation hides variation; every retrieval system has blind spots. The student who can name what their data doesn't tell them is more valuable than the student who can write the fastest query.

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Design** and query relational databases using SQL with awareness of normalization tradeoffs (Create)
2. **Model** domain knowledge as a graph (entities, relationships, properties) and query it with Cypher (Create)
3. **Implement** vector embeddings for semantic search and explain what similarity means (and doesn't mean) (Apply)
4. **Build** a hybrid retrieval pipeline combining SQL, graph traversal, and vector similarity (Create)
5. **Identify** the assumptions embedded in any data structure and articulate what the data cannot tell you (Analyze)
6. **Apply** data security practices: parameterized queries, access control, encryption, privacy principles (Apply)
7. **Produce** the Data Assumptions Document — a formal record of every assumption embedded in the data architecture (Create)
8. **Evaluate** data quality: completeness, consistency, timeliness, and the criteria that matter for specific use cases (Evaluate)
9. **Construct** a Context Pack documenting data architecture decisions, quality criteria, and known limitations (Create)

## Signature Assessment: The Data Assumptions Document

Every data architecture embodies assumptions. IS 331's distinctive artifact makes those assumptions explicit:

| Data Structure | Assumption | What this misses | Risk if wrong |
|---------------|-----------|-----------------|---------------|
| Users table has one email per user | Each person has one primary email | Shared accounts, work/personal distinction, people without email | Registration failures, duplicate accounts |
| Workshop categories are fixed enum | The set of categories won't change frequently | New categories require a schema migration | Rigidity as the organization evolves |
| Vector similarity > 0.85 means "relevant" | Cosine similarity is a good proxy for semantic relevance | Similarity can mean "related" without meaning "relevant to this query" | Confidently wrong search results |

The Data Assumptions Document is the Epistemic Humility artifact — evidence that the student can name what their data system doesn't know.

## Data Structures as Theories

A central teaching framework in IS 331: every data structure is a **theory about how the world works**.

| Data Structure | Theory it embodies | What it's good at | What it misses |
|---------------|-------------------|-------------------|---------------|
| Relational table | The world can be decomposed into entities with fixed attributes | Structured queries, consistency, ACID guarantees | Relationships between entities, unstructured data |
| Graph | The world is a network of relationships | Traversing connections, finding paths, pattern matching | Aggregation, numerical analysis, tabular reporting |
| Vector space | Meaning can be captured as position in high-dimensional space | Semantic similarity, fuzzy matching, "find more like this" | Precision, logic, negation ("find things that are NOT like this") |
| Hybrid | All of the above, combined | Complex questions that require multiple perspectives | Complexity, consistency between representations, operational overhead |

The lesson: choosing a data structure is choosing a theory. The student who understands this builds better systems and fails more gracefully.
