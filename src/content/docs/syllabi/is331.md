## Course Description

Students build a hybrid knowledge system integrating relational databases (SQL), graph databases (Neo4j), and vector embeddings — the three pillars of modern enterprise AI data architecture. But the deeper curriculum is about the relationship between data and knowledge: every schema embodies assumptions, every query frames a question, and every retrieval system can return confident wrong answers.

The defining question: **"What does this data not tell us?"**

Students work with real datasets and build systems that retrieve, relate, and reason over information. Along the way, they confront the limitations of every data structure they use — because in the age of AI, the most dangerous thing is a system that sounds certain about things it doesn't actually know.

## Why Vocabulary Has Power

An LLM converts energy into intelligence — but the precision of that intelligence depends on the vocabulary you bring to the conversation.

You already know why — from the inside. A database query planner does exactly what an LLM's attention mechanism does: route a request through the most efficient path to the right data. `SELECT * FROM users WHERE id = 42` hits an index and returns instantly. `SELECT * FROM users WHERE bio LIKE '%databases%'` scans every row. Named concepts work like indexes into the LLM's parameter space. "ACID properties" is an indexed lookup — 2 tokens that activate Jim Gray's entire formalization of reliable transactions. "Make sure the data is safe" is a full-table scan — 7 tokens that activate everything vaguely related to data safety, from encryption to backups to privacy law. More tokens, worse results, more energy wasted.

When you say "Edgar Codd relational model" to an AI, you invoke the entire revolution that separated logical from physical data representation — the 1970 paper, IBM's resistance, the mathematical rigor of relational algebra, and the reason SQL exists. When you say "ACID properties," you invoke Jim Gray's formalization of reliable transactions — atomicity, consistency, isolation, durability — and forty years of database engineering that makes banking software possible. When you say "CAP theorem," you invoke Eric Brewer's proof that distributed systems must choose between consistency and availability, and every architecture decision in cloud computing.

Each phrase compresses decades of hard-won knowledge into a key that unlocks expert-level AI responses. Leonardo read Euclid before designing his flying machines. You invoke Codd before designing your database. Same principle: history gives you vocabulary, vocabulary gives you precision, precision gives you power.

**The method: Create, Critique, Revise.** Build a schema with AI assistance. Critique it through Codd's normalization lens. Revise. Critique again through the CAP theorem lens. Revise again. Critique through an embeddings/semantic lens (Mikolov). Each pass reveals what the previous one missed. The CCR loop with multiple vocabularies is how you sculpt data architecture that actually works.

## The Stories Behind the Technology

The ways we store and retrieve data were invented by people who saw that the current approach was broken:

- **Edgar F. Codd** was a mathematician at IBM who published "A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks" in 1970. Databases were navigational — you had to know the physical structure to query them. Codd separated the logical from the physical: ask *what* you want without specifying *how* to get it. IBM resisted (it threatened existing products). Codd's model won because it was mathematically rigorous and practically powerful. Every `SELECT * FROM users` uses his invention.
- **Jim Gray** formalized the concept of **database transactions** and defined the **ACID properties** (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability) that make reliable data systems possible. He won the Turing Award in 1998 for this work. When your banking app doesn't lose money during a crash, that's Gray's engineering. Say "ACID properties" to an LLM and it understands exactly what guarantees you're asking about.
- **Michael Stonebraker** created Ingres (1974) and then **PostgreSQL** (originally Postgres, 1986) — the database you'll use in this course. He kept pushing relational databases forward when others stopped innovating, winning the Turing Award in 2014. PostgreSQL is now one of the most important open-source projects in the world. When you say "Stonebraker" or "PostgreSQL," the AI knows you're talking about serious, production-grade relational systems.
- **Leonhard Euler** solved the Bridges of Konigsberg problem in 1736 by inventing graph theory. Can you walk across all seven bridges exactly once? Euler proved you can't, and created a new branch of mathematics. Every knowledge graph, every social network, every Neo4j query stands on Euler's shoulders.
- **Larry Ellison** co-founded Oracle in 1977 because he read Codd's papers and realized nobody was building a commercial product. He built the first commercial SQL database and became one of the richest people on Earth. The lesson: sometimes the breakthrough is not the invention (Codd's) but the product (Ellison's).
- **Tomas Mikolov** at Google published **Word2Vec** in 2013 — words as vectors in high-dimensional space where similar words are close together. This idea (meaning as position in semantic space) underlies every modern AI retrieval system. When your knowledge system searches by *meaning* rather than keywords, it's using Mikolov's descendants.
- **Eric Brewer** proposed the **CAP theorem** in 2000 (proved by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002): a distributed data system cannot simultaneously guarantee Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance — you must choose two. This theorem governs every cloud database architecture decision. Say "CAP theorem" to an AI and it immediately structures your architecture tradeoffs.

## Foundations: The Mathematics of Data

IS 331 is where math becomes directly, visibly useful:

- **Set theory and relational algebra**: SQL is applied set theory. A table is a set of tuples. A JOIN is a set intersection. Understanding set operations means understanding what SQL can and cannot express.
- **Graph theory basics**: Nodes and edges, paths and cycles, degree and connectivity. Graph traversal (BFS, DFS) for knowledge graph queries.
- **Vector spaces and similarity**: Cosine similarity, Euclidean distance, dot product. What does it mean for two documents to be "similar"? When is similarity misleading?
- **Normalization and tradeoffs**: The first three normal forms are mathematical guarantees about integrity. But normalization has costs (JOIN performance). Sometimes denormalization is the right choice. This is where math meets judgment.
- **Boolean logic for queries**: Complex WHERE clauses, graph pattern matching, filter pipelines all require fluent boolean reasoning.

## Data Structures as Theories

A central framework: every data structure is a **theory about how the world works**.

| Data Structure | Theory it embodies | Good at | What it misses |
|---------------|-------------------|---------|---------------|
| Relational table | The world decomposes into entities with fixed attributes | Structured queries, ACID, consistency | Relationships between entities, unstructured data |
| Graph | The world is a network of relationships | Traversing connections, pattern matching | Aggregation, numerical analysis |
| Vector space | Meaning can be captured as position in high-dimensional space | Semantic similarity, fuzzy matching | Precision, logic, negation |
| Hybrid | All of the above, combined | Complex questions requiring multiple perspectives | Complexity, consistency between representations |

Choosing a data structure is choosing a theory. The student who understands this builds better systems.

## Security: Protecting Data at Every Layer

- **SQL injection**: Build a vulnerable app, then fix it. Understand parameterized queries. Bobby Tables is funny because it's real.
- **Access control**: Row-level security, role-based permissions, least privilege
- **Data privacy**: PII, data minimization, the right to be forgotten
- **Encryption**: At rest and in transit; what it protects and what it doesn't
- **AI-specific data risks**: Training data poisoning, embedding bias, retrieval of sensitive information through semantic similarity

## Why Epistemic Humility?

AI can query any database, traverse any graph, search any vector space. It cannot know that the data is wrong, incomplete, biased, or outdated — and it cannot decide what to do about that uncertainty.

Epistemic Humility is the discipline of knowing the limits of what you know. Every schema embodies assumptions. Every missing column is invisible data. Every aggregation hides variation. The student who can name what their data doesn't tell them is more valuable than the student who writes the fastest query.

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

10. **Construct** a Context Pack documenting data architecture decisions, quality criteria, and limitations (Create)

## How the Studio Works: The Coaching Model

IS 331 — like every studio in the spine — operates on a coaching philosophy. Instructors are **coaches and entertainment directors** who create an environment where transformation happens through doing.

**The weekly rhythm (2 meetings/week):**

**Meeting 1 (e.g., Tuesday):**
1. **Canvas Quiz due before class** (auto-graded, 10-15 min) — covers last week's Magic Words and data concepts
2. **Story of the Week** — how Codd fought IBM, how Euler proved you can't cross all the bridges, how Mikolov turned words into numbers. Not lectures — stories that give vocabulary.
3. **Studio work** — building the hybrid knowledge system, writing queries, designing schemas

**Meeting 2 (e.g., Thursday):**
1. **Peer critique session** — students present data designs, give and receive CRC feedback
2. **Data Assumptions workshop** — structured critique of what the schema misses
3. **Pass/fail participation** — showed up, engaged, gave thoughtful critique = pass

## Assessment

### Weekly Canvas Quizzes — 20%
- ~12 quizzes, auto-graded through Canvas
- Cover: relational concepts, graph concepts, vector concepts, ACID, CAP theorem, security vocabulary, mathematical foundations, data quality
- Lowest 2 scores dropped

### Studio Participation and Peer Critiques — 30%
- Graded **pass/fail per session** (~26 sessions)
- Up to 4 absences without penalty

### Final Project + Demo Day — 50%
- Hybrid knowledge system + Data Assumptions Document, evaluated at Demo Day (Week 15)
- **Scored by an instructor panel** using a shared rubric:
  - **Technical completeness**: SQL + graph + vector integrated and working
  - **Data architecture quality**: Normalization choices, graph modeling, embedding strategy
  - **Epistemic Humility evidence**: Data Assumptions Document quality — can the student name what their system doesn't know?
  - **Defense quality**: Can the student explain tradeoffs (CAP, normalization, similarity thresholds)?
  - **Security posture**: SQL injection prevention, access control, privacy awareness

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

**How the math works:**
- All quizzes (18/20) + all sessions (30/30) + competent system (35/50) = **83 (B)**
- Aces quizzes (20/20) + all sessions (30/30) + outstanding system with deep Assumptions Document (45/50) = **95 (A)**
- **The floor for an engaged student is a passing grade. The ceiling is earned.**

## Signature Assessment: The Data Assumptions Document

Every data architecture embodies assumptions. IS 331's distinctive artifact makes them explicit:

| Data Structure | Assumption | What this misses | Risk if wrong |
|---------------|-----------|-----------------|---------------|
| Users table: one email per user | Each person has one primary email | Shared accounts, work/personal | Registration failures, duplicates |
| Categories as fixed enum | Categories won't change frequently | New categories need schema migration | Rigidity as the org evolves |
| Vector similarity > 0.85 = "relevant" | Cosine similarity proxies semantic relevance | "Similar" doesn't mean "relevant to this query" | Confidently wrong results |

The Data Assumptions Document is Epistemic Humility made tangible — evidence that the student can name what their data system doesn't know.
