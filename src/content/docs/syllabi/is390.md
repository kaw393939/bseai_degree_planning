## Course Description

This studio updates the traditional systems analysis and design course for the age of AI. Students learn to decompose complex systems — including systems with AI components that behave probabilistically — into actionable requirements, design architectures that account for the whole picture (performance, reliability, cost, user impact, failure modes), and produce specifications that IS 425 will implement.

The defining question: **"What emerges when the parts interact?"**

Students work with real project briefs (from the Center for Enterprise AI or industry partners): three sentences and a spreadsheet. They must go from ambiguity to a complete systems analysis package — stakeholder map, requirements with acceptance tests, architecture document, evaluation plan, and prototype.

## Why Vocabulary Has Power

An LLM converts energy into intelligence — but the quality of that intelligence depends entirely on the vocabulary you bring to the conversation.

When you say "Conway's Law" to an AI, you invoke Melvin Conway's 1967 observation that organizations design systems mirroring their communication structures — and decades of evidence that you cannot change a system architecture without changing the team structure that builds it. When you say "Liskov Substitution Principle," you invoke Barbara Liskov's mathematical guarantee about subtype behavior — and the broader insight that good abstractions must be replaceable without breaking the system that depends on them. When you say "leverage points," you invoke Donella Meadows's hierarchy of system intervention — from the obvious (changing parameters) to the powerful (changing paradigms).

Each phrase compresses vast systems engineering wisdom into a key that unlocks expert-level AI responses. Leonardo spent months in monastery libraries reading Vitruvius's architecture treatise before designing his own buildings. You invoke Conway and Liskov before designing your systems. Same principle: history gives you vocabulary, vocabulary gives you precision, precision gives you power.

**The method: Create, Critique, Revise.** Draft architecture using one vocabulary set. Critique through Conway's Law lens: does the team structure match the architecture? Revise. Critique through Meadows's leverage points: are we changing the right thing? Revise again. Critique through Dijkstra's structured decomposition lens. Each pass through the CCR loop applies a different chisel — like Michelangelo removing marble to reveal the form within, each vocabulary reveals something the previous one missed.

## The Stories Behind the Technology

Systems thinking has its own pantheon — people who saw the whole when everyone else saw parts:

- **Grady Booch** is one of the inventors of the **Unified Modeling Language (UML)** and a pioneer of object-oriented design. His core insight: complex systems can only be understood through multiple views — no single diagram captures the whole truth. A class diagram shows structure but not behavior. A sequence diagram shows interaction but not deployment. You need all the views, held together by an architect who understands which view answers which question. Booch also co-created the **Rational Unified Process** and later joined IBM Research to work on AI architecture. His career is a lesson in Systems Thinking: the ability to zoom in on components and zoom out to see the whole.
- **Melvin Conway** published a paper in 1967 that became **Conway's Law**: "Organizations which design systems are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." This wasn't a suggestion — it was an observation about an inescapable pattern. Amazon understood this: Jeff Bezos mandated the "two-pizza team" rule — every team small enough to be fed by two pizzas — because he knew that team structure determines system architecture. When you analyze a tangled system, Conway's Law tells you to look at the org chart first.
- **Donella Meadows** was an environmental scientist and systems theorist who wrote *Thinking in Systems: A Primer* (2008, published posthumously). Her key framework: **leverage points** — the places in a system where a small change produces a big effect. Most people push the easy levers (add more resources, add more rules). The most powerful levers are the hardest to find: the goals of the system, the paradigm out of which the system arises, the power to transcend paradigms. When you analyze an AI system, Meadows's framework helps you find where a design change will actually matter.
- **Edsger Dijkstra** wrote "Go To Statement Considered Harmful" in 1968 — a one-page letter that changed programming forever. His deeper contribution was **structured programming**: the principle that complex systems must be built from well-defined, composable abstractions. He also invented the shortest-path algorithm that powers every GPS and network routing system on Earth. Dijkstra insisted that programming is a branch of mathematics and that correctness can be *proven*, not just tested. His standard: "Program testing can be used to show the presence of bugs, but never to show their absence."
- **Barbara Liskov** defined the **Liskov Substitution Principle** (1987): if you can swap a component for a subtype and the system still works, your abstraction is sound. If it breaks, your inheritance hierarchy is lying to you. This principle — part of the SOLID framework — is the mathematical test for clean architecture. Liskov won the Turing Award in 2008 for her work on data abstraction and distributed computing. When you design a system with replaceable components, you're applying Liskov.
- **Fred Brooks** (again — because his ideas span the whole spine) identified the **"second-system effect"**: the tendency to over-design your second system by cramming in all the features you left out of the first. IS 390 students are designing systems after five studios of experience. The temptation to over-engineer is real. Brooks's warning: elegance is not additional features. It's the minimum set of features that fully solves the problem.
- **Martin Fowler** wrote *Refactoring* (1999) and *Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture* (2002) — defining the vocabulary for how large-scale systems are structured. His concept of "code smells" gave developers a shared language for recognizing design problems. Fowler also championed continuous integration, microservices architecture, and the idea that architecture is not a one-time decision but an evolving conversation.
- **Jay Forrester** invented **system dynamics** at MIT in the 1950s — the mathematical framework for modeling feedback loops, delays, and nonlinear behavior in complex systems. His models of urban dynamics and global resource limits showed that systems often behave counterintuitively: the obvious solution makes things worse because it ignores feedback loops. Every AI system has feedback loops (user behavior changes the training data changes the model changes the user behavior). Forrester's work explains why.

## Foundations: The Mathematics of Systems

Systems analysis requires mathematical thinking applied to architecture:

- **Graph theory for architecture**: System architectures are graphs — components are nodes, dependencies are edges. Cycle detection reveals circular dependencies. Topological sort reveals build and deployment order. Degree centrality reveals which components are most critical (and most risky).
- **Probability for AI components**: AI components don't always give the same answer. Understanding accuracy, precision, recall, F1 — and what each metric hides — is essential for specifying AI component requirements. If your system requires 95% accuracy, what does that mean for the 5% of users who get wrong answers?
- **Queuing theory for performance**: Request arrival rates, service times, queue depths. Little's Law (L = lambda * W) tells you how many requests are in your system at any moment. These are the numbers that drive architecture decisions about scaling, caching, and load balancing.
- **Decision matrices**: Weighted scoring for architecture tradeoffs — assign criteria (performance, cost, reliability, time-to-market), weight each criterion, score each option. This is basic linear algebra applied to design decisions. The math is simple; the judgment about weights is where the real thinking happens.

## Security at the Architecture Level

IS 390 introduces security as a first-class architectural concern, not an afterthought:

- **Threat modeling**: STRIDE (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege) applied to every component in the architecture
- **Attack surface analysis**: Every interface, endpoint, and data flow is a potential attack vector. Architecture decisions expand or reduce the attack surface.
- **Defense in depth**: Multiple layers of security controls so that no single failure compromises the system. This is systems thinking applied to security — no single component can be trusted to be secure.
- **AI-specific security**: Model extraction attacks, prompt injection, data poisoning, adversarial inputs. When your architecture includes AI components, the threat model must include AI-specific threats.
- **Zero Trust architecture**: The principle that no component, user, or network connection is trusted by default. Every access must be verified. This is increasingly the standard for enterprise systems.

## The Entrepreneurial Lens on Systems

IS 390 connects systems design to business reality:

- **Build vs. buy vs. open-source**: For every component in the architecture, the decision isn't just technical — it's strategic. What's your core differentiator? Build that. What's commodity? Buy it. What's well-solved by the community? Use open-source. This is Lean thinking applied to architecture.
- **Technical debt as strategic choice**: Sometimes you deliberately take on debt (use a simpler architecture now, plan to refactor later) because time-to-market matters more than elegance. The key is being explicit: this is debt, here's the interest rate, here's when we'll pay it off. Fowler's refactoring vocabulary makes debt visible.
- **Platform thinking**: Does this system serve one client or many? The difference between a bespoke solution and a platform has enormous architectural implications. Amazon's origin as a bookstore and evolution into AWS is the ultimate platform thinking example — and Conway's Law explains how it happened.

## Why Systems Thinking?

AI can model systems it's trained on. It cannot notice that the system itself is the wrong frame.

Systems Thinking is the ability to see the whole when everyone else sees parts — to identify emergent behavior, unintended consequences, feedback loops, and second-order effects. Adding an AI component to a workflow doesn't just automate a step; it changes the incentives, failure modes, trust dynamics, and power relationships of the entire system. Only a human can stand back far enough to see that.

## How the Studio Works: The Coaching Model

IS 390 — like every studio in the spine — operates on a coaching philosophy. Instructors are **coaches and entertainment directors** who create an environment where transformation happens through doing.

**The weekly rhythm (2 meetings/week):**

**Meeting 1 (e.g., Tuesday):**
1. **Canvas Quiz due before class** (auto-graded, 10-15 min) — covers last week's Magic Words and architecture concepts
2. **Story of the Week** — how Conway discovered his Law watching IBM fail, how Dijkstra fought the `goto` lobby, how Meadows found leverage in global systems. Not lectures — stories that give vocabulary.
3. **Studio work** — stakeholder mapping, requirements writing, architecture diagramming, threat modeling, prototyping

**Meeting 2 (e.g., Thursday):**
1. **Peer critique session** — students present architecture decisions, give and receive CRC feedback
2. **Systems Decomposition workshop** — structured critique of what emerges when the parts interact
3. **Pass/fail participation** — showed up, engaged, gave thoughtful critique = pass

## Assessment

Assessment is designed to reward engagement and effort while reserving top grades for demonstrated excellence. The structure ensures that **consistent participation guarantees a passing grade**, while the A requires work that impresses a panel of faculty evaluators.

### Weekly Canvas Quizzes — 20%
- ~12 quizzes, auto-graded through Canvas
- Cover: architecture patterns (monolith, microservices, event-driven, pipeline, layered), requirements engineering, systems thinking vocabulary (emergence, feedback loops, leverage points, delays), security (STRIDE, defense in depth, Zero Trust), UML and modeling concepts, Conway's Law, Liskov Substitution, Dijkstra's structured programming, Fowler's refactoring vocabulary
- Lowest 2 scores dropped
- **Design intent:** Easy points for engaged students. They build the professional vocabulary you need to design systems and communicate with architects. If you do the reading and attend class, you'll score well.

### Studio Participation and Peer Critiques — 30%
- Graded **pass/fail per session** (~26 sessions)
- Criteria: present, engaged, gave CRC feedback to peers, participated in architecture critique
- Up to 4 absences without penalty
- **Design intent:** This gets people into the room, debating architecture, challenging each other's designs. Pass/fail means nobody agonizes over critique grades — but missing sessions costs real points.

### Final Project + Architecture Review Day — 50%
- Systems Analysis Package evaluated at Architecture Review Day (Week 15)
- **Scored by an instructor panel** (faculty + industry guests where possible): each evaluator scores independently using a shared rubric, scores are aggregated
- Rubric categories:
  - **Requirements quality**: Traceability, acceptance tests, AI-specific requirements
  - **Architecture soundness**: Component design, tradeoff analysis with decision matrices, Conway's Law awareness
  - **Threat model depth**: STRIDE coverage, AI-specific threats, defense-in-depth rationale
  - **Systems Thinking evidence**: Can the student identify emergence, feedback loops, second-order effects?
  - **Defense quality**: Can the student explain every decision? Do they handle tough questions with honesty?
- **Design intent:** This is where grade differentiation happens. A student who shows up every week and produces a functional but basic analysis earns a solid B. A student whose package is thorough, well-reasoned, and confidently defended earns the A.

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

**How the math works:**
- All quizzes (18/20) + all sessions (30/30) + competent package (35/50) = **83 (B)**
- Aces quizzes (20/20) + all sessions (30/30) + outstanding architecture with deep tradeoff analysis (45/50) = **95 (A)**
- **The floor for an engaged student is a passing grade. The ceiling is earned.**

## Key Deliverables

### Systems Analysis Package (final project)
A complete blueprint for an AI-powered system that IS 425 could implement:
- **Requirements Document**: functional and non-functional requirements with traceability to stakeholder needs, acceptance tests for each requirement
- **Architecture Document**: component diagram, data flows, integration points, technology choices with decision matrix and tradeoff analysis
- **Threat Model**: STRIDE analysis, attack surface map, security controls with defense-in-depth rationale
- **Risk Analysis**: failure modes, AI-specific risks (hallucination, drift, bias), mitigation strategies
- **Evaluation Plan**: what "good" means, how it's measured, what thresholds trigger action, what can go wrong with the evaluation itself
- **Prototype**: working demonstration of key architectural decisions

### Systems Decomposition — Human Edge Artifact
A structured analysis mapping:
- How the system's components interact to produce behavior no single component exhibits alone
- Where complexity hides — integration points, edge cases, failure modes visible only at the system level
- Feedback loops: where output becomes input, and how that changes system behavior over time
- Second-order effects: what happens when this system changes the behavior of the humans and organizations that use it
- AI-specific emergence: how probabilistic components create non-deterministic system behavior
- Leverage points: where a small design change produces the biggest improvement (Meadows framework)
- Conway's Law analysis: how the team structure shaped (or constrained) the architecture

### Architecture Review Day (Week 15)
Students present their architecture to a review panel (faculty + industry guests where possible), defending design decisions with evidence:
- Architecture walkthrough with tradeoff rationale
- Threat model and security posture defense
- Evaluation plan defense
- Response to challenge questions
- Honest assessment of what they'd change with more time/resources
