## Course Description

Students take the systems analysis from IS 390 and build it: a production-quality AI system integrating knowledge graphs, human-in-the-loop workflows, vector retrieval, evaluation harnesses, and observability — for a real client with real requirements.

The defining question: **"Who is accountable when this system fails?"**

The answer is the student. Not the AI. Not the framework. Not the team lead. The individual who built it, deployed it, and presented it at Demo Day stands up and says: *"I own this."*

Students work with real clients (from the Center for Enterprise AI or industry partners), manage expectations, deliver incrementally, run incident response when things break, and present their work to industry judges who ask hard questions.

## The Stories Behind the Technology

IS 425 is where everything converges — and the stories reflect the weight of building systems that people rely on:

- **Alan Turing** asked "Can machines think?" in 1950 and defined the framework (the Turing Test) that launched artificial intelligence as a field. But Turing's deeper contribution was the concept of **computability** — the mathematical proof that some problems are fundamentally unsolvable by any process, human or machine. When students in IS 425 encounter AI limitations, they're touching the boundary Turing defined.
- **Geoffrey Hinton**, Yann LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio spent decades working on neural networks when the rest of the field had abandoned them. Hinton's backpropagation work (1986) and deep learning breakthroughs (2006, 2012) created the technology that powers modern AI. In 2023, Hinton left Google to warn about AI risks. The lesson: the people who build powerful technology are the ones who best understand its dangers — and the ones most responsible for speaking honestly about them. That's Accountable Leadership.
- **Fei-Fei Li** created ImageNet (2009) — the massive labeled dataset that proved deep learning worked for computer vision and triggered the modern AI revolution. Her insight: AI was bottlenecked not by algorithms but by data. She organized a global effort to label 14 million images, and the resulting competition (ILSVRC) became the benchmark that drove the field forward. The lesson: sometimes the unglamorous infrastructure work (building the dataset) matters more than the clever algorithm.
- **Margaret Hamilton** led the software team for NASA's Apollo program. Her code — and her insistence on rigorous error handling — saved Apollo 11 when the computer overloaded during lunar descent. She coined the term "software engineering" because she believed software deserved the same rigor as any other engineering discipline. When IS 425 students build evaluation harnesses and error handling, they're continuing Hamilton's legacy: the belief that software must work when it matters most.

## Foundations: The Mathematics of AI Evaluation

IS 425 requires quantitative rigor in evaluating AI system performance:

- **Precision, recall, and F1**: These are not just "ML metrics" — they represent fundamental tradeoffs between false positives and false negatives. In a medical system, what's worse: missing a disease (low recall) or flagging healthy patients (low precision)? In an enterprise system, what's worse: retrieving irrelevant information (low precision) or missing relevant information (low recall)? The math is simple; the judgment about which tradeoff to make is the hard part.
- **Confusion matrices**: The visual representation of what your system gets right and wrong, cross-tabulated. Essential for communicating AI performance to clients who don't speak statistics.
- **Statistical significance**: When you change your system and accuracy goes from 87% to 89%, is that real improvement or noise? Basic hypothesis testing prevents false confidence in small improvements.
- **A/B testing and experiment design**: If you deploy a new retrieval strategy, how do you measure whether it's actually better? Controlled experiments, sample sizes, and confidence intervals.
- **Cost-benefit analysis for AI decisions**: If your system makes 1,000 decisions per day with 95% accuracy, that's 50 wrong decisions per day. What does each wrong decision cost? This arithmetic drives the case for human-in-the-loop review at critical decision points.

## Security in Production AI Systems

IS 425 treats security as a production requirement, not a checkpoint:

- **Threat modeling review**: The IS 390 threat model is validated against the actual implementation. Every identified threat must have a corresponding control, or an explicit acceptance of the risk with documented reasoning.
- **AI-specific attacks**: Prompt injection (getting the AI to ignore its instructions), model extraction (stealing the AI's behavior by querying it enough), adversarial inputs (data designed to fool the model), data poisoning (corrupting training/retrieval data).
- **Supply chain security**: Every dependency is a potential vulnerability. SBOMs (Software Bills of Materials), dependency scanning, and version pinning.
- **Observability as security**: Logging decisions, tracing data flow, alerting on anomalies — these aren't just debugging tools. They're how you detect that something malicious is happening.
- **Incident response for AI systems**: When an AI system behaves unexpectedly, how do you determine whether it's a bug, a drift issue, or an attack? The triage process differs from traditional systems.

## The Entrepreneurial Reality of Delivery

IS 425 is where entrepreneurial thinking meets execution:

- **Client management**: Real clients don't speak your language, don't know what they want until they see it, change their minds, and have budgets and timelines that don't care about your architecture preferences. Managing this relationship is a core skill.
- **Scope management and the iron triangle**: Time, quality, scope — you can control two. When the deadline is fixed and the quality bar is non-negotiable, scope is the variable. The courage to say "we need to cut this feature to ship on time" is Accountable Leadership.
- **Demo Day as investor pitch**: The Demo Day format mimics a startup pitch — you demonstrate your product, defend your decisions, acknowledge your failures, and answer hard questions from judges who've built real systems.
- **From prototype to production**: The gap between "it works on my laptop" and "it works reliably for 100 concurrent users in production" is where most projects die. IS 425 students cross that gap.

## Why Accountable Leadership?

AI cannot be sued, fired, shamed, or praised. When a system fails — when the knowledge graph has a gap that sends a wrong answer to a customer, when the HITL workflow bypasses a human review, when the evaluation harness misses a regression — someone must face the client, explain what happened, take responsibility, and commit to a fix.

Accountable Leadership is the willingness to stand behind your work, defend your decisions with evidence, acknowledge your failures honestly, and take action to improve. This is the ultimate human capability because it requires **moral agency** — something no AI system possesses or can possess.

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Build** a production-quality AI system integrating knowledge graphs, vector retrieval, and HITL workflows (Create)
2. **Design** and implement evaluation harnesses with regression gates and statistical rigor (Create)
3. **Deploy** an AI system with observability including decision tracing, failure logging, and human override tracking (Create)
4. **Present** a system to industry stakeholders at Demo Day, defending design decisions with evidence (Create)
5. **Write** honest postmortems identifying what worked, what failed, and what to improve (Evaluate)
6. **Take accountability** for system outcomes including failures, biases, and unintended consequences (Evaluate)
7. **Manage** a real client relationship including expectations, feedback, and delivery (Apply)
8. **Implement** security controls validated against the threat model from IS 390 (Apply)
9. **Construct** production-grade Context Packs synthesizing domain knowledge, system architecture, and evaluation criteria (Create)
10. **Reflect** on leadership decisions and their impact in a formal Leadership Reflection artifact (Evaluate)

## Assessment

### AI-Generated Vocabulary Quizzes (30%)

Weekly quizzes covering production AI engineering, security, and leadership concepts:

- Knowledge graph concepts (entity types, relationships, reasoning, inference)
- Evaluation concepts (precision, recall, F1, confusion matrix, regression gates, statistical significance)
- Security vocabulary (prompt injection, model extraction, SBOM, incident response, threat model validation)
- Production engineering (observability, SLIs/SLOs, deployment strategies, rollback)
- AI system architecture (HITL workflows, vector retrieval, hybrid pipelines, embedding strategies)
- Leadership concepts (accountability, blameless postmortems, stakeholder communication, scope management)

### Qualitative Studio Reviews (70%)

| Review Point | What's Evaluated |
|---|---|
| **Week 4 — Knowledge Graph + Data Integration Review** | Working KG with real domain data, initial retrieval pipeline. Can the student explain entity design decisions and what the graph misses? Security controls assessment. |
| **Week 8 — Mid-Project Client Checkpoint** | Working system demonstrated to actual client. Can the student manage client feedback professionally? Is the evaluation harness measuring real quality? Honest assessment of what's working and what isn't. |
| **Week 12 — Incident Drill + Production Review** | Response to injected failure. Security review of production deployment. Can the student detect, diagnose, and recover? Is the postmortem blameless and actionable? |
| **Week 15 — Demo Day (Industry Judges)** | Live system demonstration, architecture defense, evaluation evidence, failure cases, Q&A. Can the student stand behind their work and acknowledge its limitations honestly? Leadership Reflection quality. |

Each review documents: what the student demonstrated, feedback given, commitments for improvement, qualitative rating (Exceeds / Meets / Approaching / Not Yet).

**Demo Day Scoring:**

| Component | Weight |
|-----------|--------|
| External judging (value, clarity, evidence) | 25% |
| Faculty rubric (engineering depth, evaluation, reliability, responsible AI) | 25% |
| Technical deliverables (tests, CI, docs, security, reproducibility) | 25-35% |
| Individual contribution (peer eval + commit evidence) | 15-25% |

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

*Final grades combine quiz scores (30%) and the instructor's holistic assessment across the four studio reviews (70%), with Demo Day weighting within the final review.*

## Key Deliverables

### Production AI System
A working, deployed system meeting client requirements:

**Non-negotiable components:**
- **Knowledge graph**: entities, relationships, and reasoning over domain structure
- **Human-in-the-loop**: review points, escalation paths, override capability
- **Vector retrieval**: integrated with graph context for semantic search
- **Evaluation harness**: automated quality checks with regression gates and statistical rigor
- **Observability**: trace decisions, log failures, track human overrides
- **Security controls**: validated against IS 390 threat model

**Production standards:**
- Deployed and accessible
- CI/CD pipeline with automated testing (including security scanning)
- Structured logging and monitoring dashboards
- Runbook for operations and incident response
- Documentation: architecture, API, security posture, and user guide

### Leadership Reflection — Human Edge Artifact
A formal document reflecting on:
- Key decisions made during the project and their reasoning
- Decisions that turned out to be wrong, and what was learned
- Moments where the student chose the harder right over the easier wrong
- How accountability was practiced: client communication, error disclosure, team dynamics
- What they would do differently with the same constraints
- Turing, Hinton, Hamilton: what does their example teach about accountability in technology?

### Postmortem + Improvement Plan
A blameless postmortem analyzing:
- What worked and why
- What failed and why
- Security findings and remediation
- Specific actions for improvement (concrete, not vague)
