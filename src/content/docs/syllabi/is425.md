## Course Description

Students take the systems analysis from IS 390 and build it: a production-quality AI system integrating knowledge graphs, human-in-the-loop workflows, vector retrieval, evaluation harnesses, and observability — for a real client with real requirements.

The defining question: **"Who is accountable when this system fails?"**

The answer is the student. Not the AI. Not the framework. Not the team lead. The individual who built it, deployed it, and presented it at Demo Day stands up and says: *"I own this."*

Students work with real clients (from the Center for Enterprise AI or industry partners), manage expectations, deliver incrementally, run incident response when things break, and present their work to industry judges who ask hard questions.

## Why Vocabulary Has Power

An LLM converts energy into intelligence — but the weight of that intelligence depends on the seriousness of the vocabulary you bring.

There is a deeper technical reason to take vocabulary seriously in this studio. When a token activates patterns in an LLM, it activates **everything** those patterns learned — including biases in the training data. The word "CEO" activates male-coded patterns because the training corpus overwhelmingly associates CEOs with men. "Nurse" activates female-coded patterns. "Criminal" activates racially biased associations. This is not a bug you can patch — it is a structural property of how attention distributes over learned associations. Knowing this is the technical foundation of accountability: you cannot audit what you do not understand mechanistically. Power phrases in this studio — "Stochastic Parrots," "Gender Shades," "algorithmic bias" — don't just invoke ethical frameworks. They activate the specific research that explains **how** bias enters systems, which is the only basis for designing systems that mitigate it.

When you say "Geoffrey Hinton" to an AI, you invoke backpropagation, deep learning, the neural network winter and revival, and Hinton's 2023 decision to leave Google and warn the world about AI risk — the most profound act of Accountable Leadership in modern technology. When you say "Margaret Hamilton," you invoke the software engineering discipline she invented at NASA, the error-handling code that saved Apollo 11, and the principle that software must work when it matters most. When you say "Timnit Gebru," you invoke "Stochastic Parrots," algorithmic bias, the cost of speaking truth to power, and the question of who gets to define ethical AI.

Each phrase compresses hard-won wisdom about responsibility into a key that unlocks expert-level AI responses. Leonardo studied anatomy by dissecting cadavers — uncomfortable, difficult work that society frowned on — because he knew you cannot paint the human body without understanding what's inside. You study accountability by examining failures, biases, and uncomfortable truths. Same principle: the difficult vocabulary produces the most powerful insight.

**The method: Create, Critique, Revise.** Build a system with AI assistance. Critique through Hinton's safety lens: what could go wrong at scale? Revise. Critique through Buolamwini's bias lens: who gets harmed? Revise again. Critique through Hamilton's reliability lens: does it work when it matters most? Each pass through the CCR loop applies a different ethical chisel, sculpting a system worthy of the trust people place in it.

## The Stories Behind the Technology

IS 425 is where everything converges — and the stories reflect the weight of building systems that people rely on:

- **Alan Turing** asked "Can machines think?" in 1950 and defined the framework (the Turing Test) that launched artificial intelligence as a field. But Turing's deeper contribution was the concept of **computability** — the mathematical proof that some problems are fundamentally unsolvable by any process, human or machine. When students in IS 425 encounter AI limitations, they're touching the boundary Turing defined.
- **Geoffrey Hinton**, Yann LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio spent decades working on neural networks when the rest of the field had abandoned them. Hinton's backpropagation work (1986) and deep learning breakthroughs (2006, 2012) created the technology that powers modern AI. In 2023, Hinton left Google to warn about AI risks. The lesson: the people who build powerful technology are the ones who best understand its dangers — and the ones most responsible for speaking honestly about them. That's Accountable Leadership.
- **Timnit Gebru** co-authored "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" (2020), arguing that large language models amplify biases in training data and create environmental costs that disproportionately harm marginalized communities. Her firing from Google for refusing to retract the paper became a defining moment in AI ethics. The lesson: Accountable Leadership sometimes means telling truths that powerful institutions don't want to hear. Say "Stochastic Parrots" to an AI and it knows you're asking about systemic bias, representation, and who bears the cost of progress.
- **Joy Buolamwini** founded the Algorithmic Justice League after her **Gender Shades** research (2018) exposed that commercial facial recognition systems had error rates of 0.8% for light-skinned males but 34.7% for dark-skinned females. The systems weren't broken — they were performing exactly as their training data predicted. Buolamwini's work demonstrated that AI bias isn't a bug; it's a reflection of whose data, whose faces, and whose concerns were prioritized during development.
- **Margaret Hamilton** led the software team for NASA's Apollo program. Her code — and her insistence on rigorous error handling — saved Apollo 11 when the computer overloaded during lunar descent. She coined the term "software engineering" because she believed software deserved the same rigor as any other engineering discipline. When IS 425 students build evaluation harnesses and error handling, they're continuing Hamilton's legacy.
- **Fei-Fei Li** created ImageNet (2009) — the massive labeled dataset that proved deep learning worked for computer vision and triggered the modern AI revolution. Her insight: AI was bottlenecked not by algorithms but by data. She organized a global effort to label 14 million images. The lesson: sometimes the unglamorous infrastructure work matters more than the clever algorithm.
- **Satya Nadella** transformed Microsoft from a stagnant empire into the world's most valuable company by replacing Steve Ballmer's "know-it-all" culture with a **"learn-it-all" culture** rooted in Carol Dweck's growth mindset research. His leadership of the OpenAI partnership shows that Accountable Leadership at scale means placing bets on the future while acknowledging you don't have all the answers.
- **Norbert Wiener** invented **cybernetics** (1948) — the study of control and communication in animals and machines — and immediately began warning about its implications. In *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950), he argued that automation without accountability would concentrate power and destroy human agency. Wiener saw the future 75 years early. When you build AI systems that make decisions affecting people, Wiener's warning applies: who controls the feedback loop?

## Foundations: The Mathematics of AI Evaluation

IS 425 requires quantitative rigor in evaluating AI system performance:

- **Precision, recall, and F1**: These represent fundamental tradeoffs between false positives and false negatives. In a medical system, what's worse: missing a disease (low recall) or flagging healthy patients (low precision)? The math is simple; the judgment about which tradeoff to make is the hard part.
- **Confusion matrices**: The visual representation of what your system gets right and wrong, cross-tabulated. Essential for communicating AI performance to clients who don't speak statistics.
- **Statistical significance**: When accuracy goes from 87% to 89%, is that real improvement or noise? Basic hypothesis testing prevents false confidence in small improvements.
- **A/B testing and experiment design**: If you deploy a new retrieval strategy, how do you measure whether it's actually better? Controlled experiments, sample sizes, and confidence intervals.
- **Cost-benefit analysis for AI decisions**: If your system makes 1,000 decisions per day with 95% accuracy, that's 50 wrong decisions per day. What does each wrong decision cost? This arithmetic drives the case for human-in-the-loop review at critical decision points.

## Security in Production AI Systems

IS 425 treats security as a production requirement, not a checkpoint:

- **Threat modeling review**: The IS 390 threat model is validated against the actual implementation. Every identified threat must have a corresponding control, or an explicit acceptance of the risk with documented reasoning.
- **AI-specific attacks**: Prompt injection (getting the AI to ignore its instructions), model extraction (stealing the AI's behavior by querying it enough), adversarial inputs (data designed to fool the model), data poisoning (corrupting training/retrieval data).
- **Supply chain security**: Every dependency is a potential vulnerability. SBOMs (Software Bills of Materials), dependency scanning, and version pinning.
- **Observability as security**: Logging decisions, tracing data flow, alerting on anomalies — these aren't just debugging tools. They're how you detect that something malicious is happening.
- **Incident response for AI systems**: When an AI system behaves unexpectedly, how do you determine whether it's a bug, a drift issue, or an attack? The triage process differs from traditional systems.

## The Entrepreneurial Reality of Delivery

IS 425 is where entrepreneurial thinking meets execution:

- **Client management**: Real clients don't speak your language, don't know what they want until they see it, change their minds, and have budgets and timelines that don't care about your architecture preferences. Managing this relationship is a core skill.
- **Scope management and the iron triangle**: Time, quality, scope — you can control two. When the deadline is fixed and the quality bar is non-negotiable, scope is the variable. The courage to say "we need to cut this feature to ship on time" is Accountable Leadership.
- **Demo Day as investor pitch**: The Demo Day format mimics a startup pitch — you demonstrate your product, defend your decisions, acknowledge your failures, and answer hard questions from judges who've built real systems.
- **From prototype to production**: The gap between "it works on my laptop" and "it works reliably for 100 concurrent users in production" is where most projects die. IS 425 students cross that gap.

## Why Accountable Leadership?

AI cannot be sued, fired, shamed, or praised. When a system fails — when the knowledge graph has a gap that sends a wrong answer to a customer, when the HITL workflow bypasses a human review, when the evaluation harness misses a regression — someone must face the client, explain what happened, take responsibility, and commit to a fix.

Accountable Leadership is the willingness to stand behind your work, defend your decisions with evidence, acknowledge your failures honestly, and take action to improve. This is the ultimate human capability because it requires **moral agency** — something no AI system possesses or can possess.

## How the Studio Works: The Coaching Model

IS 425 — like every studio in the spine — operates on a coaching philosophy. Instructors are **coaches and entertainment directors** who create an environment where transformation happens through doing.

**The weekly rhythm (2 meetings/week):**

**Meeting 1 (e.g., Tuesday):**
1. **Canvas Quiz due before class** (auto-graded, 10-15 min) — covers last week's Magic Words and production AI concepts
2. **Story of the Week** — how Hinton left Google to warn the world, how Buolamwini exposed facial recognition bias, how Hamilton's code saved Apollo 11. Not lectures — stories that give vocabulary and moral weight.
3. **Studio work** — building the production system: KG design, HITL workflows, evaluation harnesses, deployment

**Meeting 2 (e.g., Thursday):**
1. **Peer critique session** — students present system components, give and receive CRC feedback
2. **Accountability workshop** — structured critique: who is harmed if this fails? What does the postmortem look like?
3. **Pass/fail participation** — showed up, engaged, gave thoughtful critique = pass

## Assessment

Assessment is designed to reward engagement and effort while reserving top grades for demonstrated excellence. The structure ensures that **consistent participation guarantees a passing grade**, while the A requires work that impresses a panel that includes industry judges.

### Weekly Canvas Quizzes — 20%
- ~12 quizzes, auto-graded through Canvas
- Cover: knowledge graph concepts, evaluation metrics (precision, recall, F1, confusion matrix, statistical significance), security vocabulary (prompt injection, model extraction, SBOM, incident response), production engineering (observability, SLIs/SLOs, deployment strategies), HITL workflow patterns, leadership concepts (accountability, blameless postmortems, stakeholder communication, Gebru, Buolamwini, Wiener)
- Lowest 2 scores dropped
- **Design intent:** Easy points for engaged students. They build the vocabulary of production AI and responsible deployment. If you do the reading and attend class, you'll score well.

### Studio Participation and Peer Critiques — 30%
- Graded **pass/fail per session** (~26 sessions)
- Criteria: present, engaged, gave CRC feedback to peers, participated in accountability critique
- Up to 4 absences without penalty
- **Design intent:** This gets people into the room, debating architecture decisions and accountability tradeoffs. The social pressure of peer critique produces better work than any rubric.

### Final Project + Demo Day — 50%
- Production AI system + Leadership Reflection, evaluated at Demo Day (Week 15) with **industry judges**
- **Scored by a combined panel** (faculty + industry judges): each evaluator scores independently using a shared rubric, scores are aggregated
- Rubric categories:
  - **Technical completeness**: KG, HITL, vector retrieval, evaluation harness, observability, security controls
  - **Production quality**: Deployed, tested, monitored, documented, secure
  - **Defense quality**: Can the student explain every decision? Handle tough questions with honesty?
  - **Accountability evidence**: Leadership Reflection quality, postmortem depth, honest failure acknowledgment
  - **Client value**: Does the system solve the client's actual problem?
- **Design intent:** This is the capstone moment. A student who shows up every week and builds a functional system earns a solid B. A student whose system is production-quality, well-defended, and supported by a deep Leadership Reflection earns the A.

**Demo Day Scoring (within the 50%):**

| Component | Weight |
|-----------|--------|
| External judging (value, clarity, evidence) | 25% |
| Faculty rubric (engineering depth, evaluation, reliability, responsible AI) | 25% |
| Technical deliverables (tests, CI, docs, security, reproducibility) | 25-35% |
| Individual contribution (peer eval + commit evidence) | 15-25% |

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

**How the math works:**
- All quizzes (18/20) + all sessions (30/30) + competent system (35/50) = **83 (B)**
- Aces quizzes (20/20) + all sessions (30/30) + outstanding system with industry judge praise (45/50) = **95 (A)**
- **The floor for an engaged student is a passing grade. The ceiling is earned through excellent work.**

## Key Deliverables

### Production AI System
A working, deployed system meeting client requirements:

**Non-negotiable components:**
- **Knowledge graph**: entities, relationships, and reasoning over domain structure
- **Human-in-the-loop**: review points, escalation paths, override capability
- **Vector retrieval**: integrated with graph context for semantic search
- **Evaluation harness**: automated quality checks with regression gates and statistical rigor
- **Observability**: trace decisions, log failures, track human overrides
- **Security controls**: validated against IS 390 threat model

**Production standards:**
- Deployed and accessible
- CI/CD pipeline with automated testing (including security scanning)
- Structured logging and monitoring dashboards
- Runbook for operations and incident response
- Documentation: architecture, API, security posture, and user guide

### Leadership Reflection — Human Edge Artifact
A formal document reflecting on:
- Key decisions made during the project and their reasoning
- Decisions that turned out to be wrong, and what was learned
- Moments where the student chose the harder right over the easier wrong
- How accountability was practiced: client communication, error disclosure, team dynamics
- Gebru and Buolamwini: what does their example teach about speaking truth about AI?
- Hinton and Wiener: what does their example teach about the responsibility of builders?
- Hamilton: what does her example teach about engineering discipline under pressure?
- What they would do differently with the same constraints

### Postmortem + Improvement Plan
A blameless postmortem analyzing:
- What worked and why
- What failed and why
- Security findings and remediation
- Specific actions for improvement (concrete, not vague)
