# IS 482 — Community AI Training

**Credits:** 3 | **Contact hours:** 3 hrs/week | **Mode:** Studio (face-to-face, service learning)
**Human Edge capability:** Translation
**40/60 split:** Manual curriculum design, audience research, hands-on exercise creation / AI-assisted material generation, logistics planning, impact analysis
**Prerequisites:** IS 425

<Aside type="tip" title="Studio 8">
This is **Studio 8** — the final studio. You've built production AI systems, led teams, presented to industry judges. Now you prove you truly understand it all by teaching it to someone who has never used AI. If you can make the invisible visible, you've mastered the Human Edge.
</Aside>

## Course Description

Students take everything they've learned across seven studios and translate it into a live community AI training event. Working through the Center for Enterprise AI, students research a non-technical audience, design curriculum, build hands-on materials, deliver training sessions, collect feedback, and measure impact.

The defining question: **"Can you make the invisible visible?"**

AI is reshaping every industry, but most people outside technology have no mental model for how it works, what it can do, what it cannot do, or how to use it responsibly. The ability to bridge that gap — to translate complex technical knowledge into accessible, actionable understanding for any audience — is the ultimate proof of mastery.

This is a **service learning** course. Students serve the Newark community while demonstrating the deepest form of technical understanding: the ability to teach.

---

## Why Translation?

Every other Human Edge capability is about what you can do with AI. Translation is about what you can help *others* do.

Consider: AI companies spend billions building models, but adoption stalls because the people who need AI most — small business owners, nonprofit managers, community organizations, healthcare workers — cannot see how it applies to their actual problems. The bottleneck is not technology. The bottleneck is translation.

Translation is not "dumbing it down." It is the sophisticated cognitive work of:
- Understanding what your audience already knows (and doesn't)
- Finding the right analogies, examples, and entry points
- Designing exercises that produce genuine "aha" moments
- Adapting in real-time when your explanation isn't landing
- Measuring whether learning actually happened

This is the capability that makes every other capability valuable. If you can build a production AI system but cannot explain to a client why it matters, your system won't get deployed. If you can evaluate AI quality but cannot translate your evaluation into language a board understands, your judgment goes unheard.

Translation is what closes the loop between technical mastery and real-world impact.

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Research** a non-technical audience's AI knowledge gaps and design workshop curricula to address them (Create)
2. **Design** hands-on exercises that make abstract AI concepts accessible to diverse audiences (Create)
3. **Deliver** live training sessions, adapting in real-time to audience questions and confusion (Apply)
4. **Write** a Translation Brief documenting what it took to make AI concepts accessible to a non-technical audience (Evaluate)
5. **Plan** event logistics including venue, outreach, registration, accessibility, and contingency (Apply)
6. **Collect** and analyze participant feedback to assess community impact (Analyze)
7. **Produce** a Community Impact Report with evidence of learning outcomes (Create)
8. **Construct** Translation-focused Context Packs that bridge technical and non-technical perspectives (Create)
9. **Reflect** on the gap between expert understanding and public comprehension of AI (Evaluate)

## Key Deliverables

### Community AI Training Event (25%)
A live, public training event delivered to a real community audience through the Center for Enterprise AI:

**Non-negotiable components:**
- **Real audience**: Community members, nonprofit staff, small business owners, or other non-technical participants — not classmates
- **Hands-on exercises**: Participants use AI tools to solve problems *they* care about during the session
- **Adaptive facilitation**: Students must respond to real questions, confusion, and skepticism in real-time
- **Accessible design**: Materials must work for diverse skill levels, languages, and technological access
- **Ethical framing**: Honest discussion of AI limitations, risks, and responsible use — not hype

**Event standards:**
- Minimum 15 community participants (coordinated with Center for Enterprise AI)
- Pre-event survey to assess baseline AI knowledge
- Post-event survey to measure learning and confidence change
- Session recorded (with permission) for reflection and improvement
- All materials publicly available for future community use

### Training Curriculum and Materials (20%)
A complete, reusable training package:
- Workshop outline with learning objectives and timing
- Slide deck or visual aids designed for non-technical audiences
- Hands-on exercise guides with step-by-step instructions
- Facilitator notes for future instructors
- Troubleshooting guide for common participant issues
- All materials tested with a pilot audience before the live event

### Translation Brief — Human Edge Artifact (15%)
A formal document reflecting on the translation process:
- What the audience knew before the training and what gaps existed
- What analogies, examples, and explanations worked — and which failed
- Where the "curse of knowledge" struck: moments where expertise made communication harder
- How the student adapted when their planned explanation didn't land
- What this experience revealed about the gap between technical knowledge and public understanding
- How translation will inform the student's professional practice

This is the Translation artifact — the final Human Edge document in the program, closing the loop that began with Disciplined Inquiry in IS 117.

### Community Impact Report (15%)
An evidence-based assessment of the training's impact:
- Pre/post survey analysis showing measurable change in AI understanding
- Participant quotes and qualitative feedback
- Assessment of which learning objectives were met and which need improvement
- Recommendations for future training iterations
- Honest analysis of what didn't work and why
- Connection to broader AI literacy goals for the Newark community

### Audience Research and Needs Analysis (10%)
Before designing anything, students must understand their audience:
- Demographics: Who will attend? What are their jobs, concerns, and aspirations?
- Current AI knowledge: What do participants already know (or think they know) about AI?
- Pain points: What problems could AI help solve for this specific audience?
- Barriers: Access to technology, language, digital literacy, trust
- Cultural context: What does AI mean to this community? What are the fears, hopes, and misconceptions?

## The 40/60 Split in Practice

At Studio 8 level, the 40/60 split reflects the reality that teaching requires both human insight and efficient production:

| Component | Hard-Way (40%) | Agentic (60%) |
|-----------|---------------|---------------|
| Audience research | In-person interviews; direct community engagement; empathy mapping | AI-assisted survey analysis; demographic research synthesis |
| Curriculum design | Manual learning objective design; exercise prototyping by hand; pedagogical reasoning | AI-directed material generation; slide creation; exercise scaffolding |
| Event logistics | Venue coordination; community partner relationships; accessibility planning | AI-assisted scheduling, outreach copy, registration management |
| Impact analysis | Direct coding of qualitative feedback; honest interpretation of results | AI-assisted survey analysis; statistical summaries; visualization |

The hard-way component ensures students truly understand their audience and make genuine human connections. The agentic component ensures they can produce professional-quality materials and scale their impact.

## What Graduates Take Into Their Careers

What students take forward from IS 482 is the most portable capability of all:

- The ability to explain complex AI systems to any stakeholder — C-suite, clients, regulators, community members
- The judgment to know what to simplify and what to keep precise
- The empathy to meet audiences where they are, not where you wish they were
- The design skills to create training and communication that produce genuine understanding
- The humility to recognize that expertise means nothing if you cannot share it

These capabilities make graduates uniquely valuable in every AI role:
- **Client-facing engineers** who can translate system capabilities into business value
- **Product managers** who bridge technical teams and non-technical stakeholders
- **AI governance leads** who make policy recommendations that decision-makers understand
- **Consultants** who can assess, implement, and explain AI solutions to diverse organizations
- **Community leaders** who bring AI literacy to underserved populations

Translation is what turns a technologist into a leader.
