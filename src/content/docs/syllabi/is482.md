# IS 482 — Community AI Training

**Credits:** 3 | **Contact hours:** 3 hrs/week | **Mode:** Studio (face-to-face, service learning)
**Human Edge capability:** Translation
**40/60 split:** Manual curriculum design, audience research, hands-on exercise creation / AI-assisted material generation, logistics planning, impact analysis
**Prerequisites:** IS 425

## Course Description

Students take everything they've learned across seven studios and translate it into a live community AI training event. Working through the Center for Enterprise AI, students research a non-technical audience, design curriculum, build hands-on materials, deliver training sessions, collect feedback, and measure impact.

The defining question: **"Can you make the invisible visible?"**

AI is reshaping every industry, but most people outside technology have no mental model for how it works, what it can do, what it cannot do, or how to use it responsibly. The ability to bridge that gap — to translate complex technical knowledge into accessible, actionable understanding for any audience — is the ultimate proof of mastery.

This is a **service learning** course. Students serve the Newark community while demonstrating the deepest form of technical understanding: the ability to teach.

## The Stories Behind the Idea

Teaching and communication have their own heroes — people who proved that making knowledge accessible is as important as creating it:

- **Richard Feynman** was a Nobel Prize-winning physicist who could explain quantum mechanics to a roomful of undergraduates — or to a congressional committee. His technique: start with what the audience knows, build analogies from everyday experience, and never use a technical term without first explaining it in plain language. **"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough"** (often attributed to Einstein, but Feynman lived it). When IS 482 students design training for non-technical audiences, they're practicing Feynman's method.
- **Carl Sagan** brought astronomy to millions through *Cosmos* (1980) — the most-watched science series at the time. Sagan didn't dumb things down. He found the metaphors that made cosmic scale feel personal: "We are made of star stuff." He showed that translation is not simplification — it's finding the bridge between what you know and what your audience can imagine.
- **Paulo Freire** wrote *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968), arguing that education should not be "banking" (depositing knowledge into passive minds) but dialogue — where teacher and learner co-create understanding. Freire's work was born in the literacy programs of rural Brazil, where he taught peasant farmers to read by connecting words to their lived experience. When IS 482 students design community workshops, Freire's insight applies: start with what the community cares about, not what you want to teach.
- **Grace Hopper** was a Navy rear admiral and computer scientist who invented the first compiler and helped develop COBOL — a programming language designed to be readable by non-programmers. Her most famous demonstration: handing out "nanosecond" wires (11.8 inches of wire, the distance light travels in one nanosecond) to make abstract computer speed tangible. Hopper understood that making technology accessible is itself a technical achievement.

## Foundations: The Science of Learning and Communication

IS 482 builds on practical communication science:

- **Cognitive load theory**: Working memory can hold about 4 items at a time. When you pile jargon on a non-technical audience, you're overloading their working memory. Good curriculum design manages cognitive load: introduce one concept at a time, provide worked examples, reduce extraneous information.
- **The curse of knowledge**: Once you know something, you can't remember what it was like not to know it. You unconsciously assume your audience shares your background. IS 482 students confront this directly: the things that feel obvious to you after seven studios are completely invisible to your audience.
- **Analogies as cognitive bridges**: "A knowledge graph is like a map of everything we know about a topic, with connections between the pieces" — this analogy loses precision but gains understanding. The art is knowing which precision to sacrifice.
- **Assessment for learning**: Pre/post surveys, formative checks during the session ("thumbs up if this makes sense"), and follow-up interviews. Good teaching requires evidence of learning, not just evidence of presenting.
- **Basic statistics for impact measurement**: Pre/post score comparison, confidence intervals for small samples, qualitative coding of open-ended responses. Not deep statistics — but enough to write a credible impact report.

## Security Literacy for Everyone

A critical part of community AI training is helping people protect themselves:

- **Phishing and social engineering**: How attackers use AI to create more convincing scams. What to look for. Why "verify the source" matters more than ever.
- **Privacy in AI interactions**: When you use ChatGPT, where does your data go? What should you never type into an AI tool? How do data collection practices differ across platforms?
- **AI-generated misinformation**: Deepfakes, synthetic text, voice cloning. How to verify information in an age where seeing is no longer believing. Reverse image search, source verification, the "who benefits?" heuristic.
- **Password and account security**: The basics that still protect most people most of the time — strong unique passwords, two-factor authentication, password managers. Students teach these skills because their audience needs them.
- **Informed consent for AI tools**: When your workplace adopts AI, what questions should you ask? What data is being collected? Who has access? What decisions is it making about you?

Teaching security to non-technical audiences is one of the most valuable services IS 482 students can provide.

## Why Translation?

Every other Human Edge capability is about what you can do with AI. Translation is about what you can help *others* do.

Consider: AI companies spend billions building models, but adoption stalls because the people who need AI most — small business owners, nonprofit managers, community organizations, healthcare workers — cannot see how it applies to their actual problems. The bottleneck is not technology. The bottleneck is translation.

Translation is not "dumbing it down." It is the sophisticated cognitive work of:
- Understanding what your audience already knows (and doesn't)
- Finding the right analogies, examples, and entry points
- Designing exercises that produce genuine "aha" moments
- Adapting in real-time when your explanation isn't landing
- Measuring whether learning actually happened

This is the capability that makes every other capability valuable. If you can build a production AI system but cannot explain to a client why it matters, your system won't get deployed. If you can evaluate AI quality but cannot translate your evaluation into language a board understands, your judgment goes unheard.

Translation is what closes the loop between technical mastery and real-world impact.

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Research** a non-technical audience's AI knowledge gaps and design workshop curricula to address them (Create)
2. **Design** hands-on exercises that make abstract AI concepts accessible to diverse audiences (Create)
3. **Deliver** live training sessions, adapting in real-time to audience questions and confusion (Apply)
4. **Write** a Translation Brief documenting what it took to make AI concepts accessible to a non-technical audience (Evaluate)
5. **Teach** security awareness — phishing, privacy, misinformation, account safety — to community members (Apply)
6. **Plan** event logistics including venue, outreach, registration, accessibility, and contingency (Apply)
7. **Collect** and analyze participant feedback using pre/post surveys and qualitative methods to assess community impact (Analyze)
8. **Produce** a Community Impact Report with evidence of learning outcomes (Create)
9. **Construct** Translation-focused Context Packs that bridge technical and non-technical perspectives (Create)
10. **Reflect** on the gap between expert understanding and public comprehension of AI (Evaluate)

## Assessment

### AI-Generated Vocabulary Quizzes (30%)

Weekly quizzes covering communication science, teaching methodology, and community engagement:

- Cognitive science (cognitive load, working memory, the curse of knowledge, transfer)
- Pedagogical concepts (formative assessment, scaffolding, differentiation, Freire's banking vs. dialogue)
- Communication techniques (analogies, metaphors, audience analysis, adaptive facilitation)
- Security literacy (phishing, privacy, misinformation, verification, social engineering)
- Community engagement (needs assessment, cultural context, accessibility, informed consent)
- Impact measurement (pre/post design, qualitative coding, sample size considerations)

### Qualitative Studio Reviews (70%)

| Review Point | What's Evaluated |
|---|---|
| **Week 4 — Audience Research Review** | Needs analysis, audience profile, knowledge gap identification. Can the student explain what their audience knows, doesn't know, and fears about AI? Does the plan include security literacy? |
| **Week 8 — Pilot Session Review** | Curriculum draft, exercise prototypes, pilot delivery to classmates. Can the student adapt when their explanation doesn't land? Is the cognitive load manageable for a non-technical audience? |
| **Week 12 — Live Event Review** | Community training event delivery. Did real community members attend, participate, and learn? Was the facilitation adaptive? Was security covered? Evidence from observation and participant feedback. |
| **Week 15 — Showcase + Impact Review** | Community Impact Report, Translation Brief, final materials. Can the student demonstrate measurable impact? Can they honestly reflect on the curse of knowledge and how they overcame (or didn't overcome) it? |

Each review documents: what the student demonstrated, feedback given, commitments for improvement, qualitative rating (Exceeds / Meets / Approaching / Not Yet).

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

*Final grades combine quiz scores (30%) and the instructor's holistic assessment across the four studio reviews (70%).*

## Key Deliverables

### Community AI Training Event (live delivery)
A live, public training event delivered to a real community audience through the Center for Enterprise AI:

**Non-negotiable components:**
- **Real audience**: Community members, nonprofit staff, small business owners, or other non-technical participants — not classmates
- **Hands-on exercises**: Participants use AI tools to solve problems *they* care about during the session
- **Security literacy**: At least one session segment on protecting yourself in the age of AI (phishing, privacy, misinformation)
- **Adaptive facilitation**: Students must respond to real questions, confusion, and skepticism in real-time
- **Accessible design**: Materials must work for diverse skill levels, languages, and technological access
- **Ethical framing**: Honest discussion of AI limitations, risks, and responsible use — not hype

**Event standards:**
- Minimum 15 community participants (coordinated with Center for Enterprise AI)
- Pre-event survey to assess baseline AI knowledge
- Post-event survey to measure learning and confidence change
- Session recorded (with permission) for reflection and improvement
- All materials publicly available for future community use

### Training Curriculum and Materials
A complete, reusable training package:
- Workshop outline with learning objectives and timing
- Slide deck or visual aids designed for non-technical audiences (Grace Hopper's nanosecond wire principle: make the abstract tangible)
- Hands-on exercise guides with step-by-step instructions
- Security awareness module
- Facilitator notes for future instructors
- Troubleshooting guide for common participant issues
- All materials tested with a pilot audience before the live event

### Translation Brief — Human Edge Artifact
A formal document reflecting on the translation process:
- What the audience knew before the training and what gaps existed
- What analogies, examples, and explanations worked — and which failed
- Where the "curse of knowledge" struck: moments where expertise made communication harder
- How the student adapted when their planned explanation didn't land
- Security topics: what was hardest to make accessible? What metaphors worked?
- What this experience revealed about the gap between technical knowledge and public understanding
- How translation will inform the student's professional practice

This is the Translation artifact — the final Human Edge document in the program, closing the loop that began with Disciplined Inquiry in IS 117.

### Community Impact Report
An evidence-based assessment of the training's impact:
- Pre/post survey analysis showing measurable change in AI understanding and security awareness
- Participant quotes and qualitative feedback (coded and analyzed, not just listed)
- Assessment of which learning objectives were met and which need improvement
- Recommendations for future training iterations
- Honest analysis of what didn't work and why
- Connection to broader AI literacy goals for the Newark community
- Basic statistical analysis (confidence intervals where appropriate)

## The 40/60 Split in Practice

At Studio 8 level, the 40/60 split reflects the reality that teaching requires both human insight and efficient production:

| Component | Hard-Way (40%) | Agentic (60%) |
|-----------|---------------|---------------|
| Audience research | In-person interviews; direct community engagement; empathy mapping | AI-assisted survey analysis; demographic research synthesis |
| Curriculum design | Manual learning objective design; exercise prototyping by hand; pedagogical reasoning | AI-directed material generation; slide creation; exercise scaffolding |
| Security content | Manual research on current threats; hand-designed exercises for non-technical audiences | AI-assisted threat landscape research; material generation |
| Event logistics | Venue coordination; community partner relationships; accessibility planning | AI-assisted scheduling, outreach copy, registration management |
| Impact analysis | Direct coding of qualitative feedback; honest interpretation of results | AI-assisted survey analysis; statistical summaries; visualization |

The hard-way component ensures students truly understand their audience and make genuine human connections. The agentic component ensures they can produce professional-quality materials and scale their impact.
