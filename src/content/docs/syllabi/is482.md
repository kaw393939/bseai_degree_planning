## Course Description

Students take everything they've learned across seven studios and translate it into a live community AI training event. Working through the Center for Enterprise AI, students research a non-technical audience, design curriculum, build hands-on materials, deliver training sessions, collect feedback, and measure impact.

The defining question: **"Can you make the invisible visible?"**

AI is reshaping every industry, but most people outside technology have no mental model for how it works, what it can do, what it cannot do, or how to use it responsibly. The ability to bridge that gap — to translate complex technical knowledge into accessible, actionable understanding for any audience — is the ultimate proof of mastery.

This is a **service learning** course. Students serve the Newark community while demonstrating the deepest form of technical understanding: the ability to teach.

## Why Vocabulary Has Power

An LLM converts energy into intelligence — and in this final studio, you learn that the most powerful use of that intelligence is making it accessible to everyone.

There is a mirror image of token economics that governs this course. An LLM has a context window measured in tokens — a maximum capacity for processing information at once. Your audience has a biological context window described by John Sweller's cognitive load theory: working memory holds roughly four items before something gets pushed out. Every piece of jargon you use costs your audience cognitive tokens. The compression problem reverses: in Studios 1-7, you learned to use dense technical vocabulary to maximize what the AI gives you. In Studio 8, you learn to **decompress** — to translate dense vocabulary into terms your audience can absorb without overloading their bandwidth. Same information theory, opposite direction. Shannon's encoding theorem applies both ways.

When you say "Richard Feynman" to an AI, you invoke the Nobel physicist who could explain quantum mechanics to freshmen — and the principle that if you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it. When you say "cognitive load theory," you invoke John Sweller's finding that working memory holds about four items — and every piece of jargon you pile on your audience pushes something else out. When you say "curse of knowledge," you invoke the cognitive bias that makes experts terrible teachers — because once you know something, you can't remember what it was like not to know it.

Each phrase compresses communication science into a key that unlocks expert-level AI responses for designing training materials and workshops. Leonardo didn't just paint — he wrote notebooks filled with diagrams, analogies, and explanations designed to make his ideas accessible to future readers. His genius was not only in creation but in translation. You follow the same path: everything you've built across seven studios is only valuable if you can help others understand it.

**The method: Create, Critique, Revise — but now the critic is your audience.** Draft a workshop using one vocabulary set. Deliver it to a pilot audience. Watch their faces. Note where confusion appears. Revise. Test a different analogy. Watch again. Each pass through the CCR loop doesn't just improve your materials — it deepens your own understanding, because teaching is the most demanding form of knowing.

## The Stories Behind the Idea

Teaching and communication have their own heroes — people who proved that making knowledge accessible is as important as creating it:

- **Richard Feynman** was a Nobel Prize-winning physicist who could explain quantum mechanics to a roomful of undergraduates — or to a congressional committee. His technique: start with what the audience knows, build analogies from everyday experience, and never use a technical term without first explaining it in plain language. **"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough"** — Feynman lived it. When IS 482 students design training for non-technical audiences, they're practicing Feynman's method.
- **Carl Sagan** brought astronomy to millions through *Cosmos* (1980) — the most-watched science series at the time. Sagan didn't dumb things down. He found the metaphors that made cosmic scale feel personal: "We are made of star stuff." He showed that translation is not simplification — it's finding the bridge between what you know and what your audience can imagine.
- **Bret Victor** gave the talk "Inventing on Principle" (2012) and dedicated his career to **making the invisible visible**. His principle: creators need immediate feedback — you should see the result of every change as you make it. Victor built interactive visualizations that made abstract algorithms tangible. His work at Dynamicland explored computing you can touch. When IS 482 students design hands-on exercises that make AI concepts concrete, they're applying Victor's principle: if people can't see it, they can't understand it.
- **Paulo Freire** wrote *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968), arguing that education should not be "banking" (depositing knowledge into passive minds) but dialogue — where teacher and learner co-create understanding. Freire's work was born in the literacy programs of rural Brazil, where he taught peasant farmers to read by connecting words to their lived experience. When IS 482 students design community workshops, Freire's insight applies: start with what the community cares about, not what you want to teach.
- **Grace Hopper** was a Navy rear admiral and computer scientist who invented the first compiler and helped develop COBOL — a programming language designed to be readable by non-programmers. Her most famous demonstration: handing out "nanosecond" wires (11.8 inches of wire, the distance light travels in one nanosecond) to make abstract computer speed tangible. Hopper understood that making technology accessible is itself a technical achievement.
- **Mitch Resnick** created **Scratch** at MIT — the visual programming language used by tens of millions of children worldwide. His research group, Lifelong Kindergarten, is built on the principle that the best learning happens through creative play. Scratch didn't simplify programming — it changed the representation so that the concepts remained real but the barriers to entry disappeared. When IS 482 students design exercises for non-technical audiences, Resnick's approach applies: change the representation, not the rigor.

## Foundations: The Science of Learning and Communication

IS 482 builds on practical communication science:

- **Cognitive load theory**: Working memory can hold about 4 items at a time. When you pile jargon on a non-technical audience, you're overloading their working memory. Good curriculum design manages cognitive load: introduce one concept at a time, provide worked examples, reduce extraneous information.
- **The curse of knowledge**: Once you know something, you can't remember what it was like not to know it. You unconsciously assume your audience shares your background. IS 482 students confront this directly: the things that feel obvious after seven studios are completely invisible to your audience.
- **Analogies as cognitive bridges**: "A knowledge graph is like a map of everything we know about a topic, with connections between the pieces" — this analogy loses precision but gains understanding. The art is knowing which precision to sacrifice.
- **Assessment for learning**: Pre/post surveys, formative checks during the session ("thumbs up if this makes sense"), and follow-up interviews. Good teaching requires evidence of learning, not just evidence of presenting.
- **Basic statistics for impact measurement**: Pre/post score comparison, confidence intervals for small samples, qualitative coding of open-ended responses. Not deep statistics — but enough to write a credible impact report.

## Security Literacy for Everyone

A critical part of community AI training is helping people protect themselves:

- **Phishing and social engineering**: How attackers use AI to create more convincing scams. What to look for. Why "verify the source" matters more than ever.
- **Privacy in AI interactions**: When you use ChatGPT, where does your data go? What should you never type into an AI tool? How do data collection practices differ across platforms?
- **AI-generated misinformation**: Deepfakes, synthetic text, voice cloning. How to verify information in an age where seeing is no longer believing. Reverse image search, source verification, the "who benefits?" heuristic.
- **Password and account security**: Strong unique passwords, two-factor authentication, password managers. Students teach these skills because their audience needs them.
- **Informed consent for AI tools**: When your workplace adopts AI, what questions should you ask? What data is being collected? Who has access? What decisions is it making about you?

Teaching security to non-technical audiences is one of the most valuable services IS 482 students can provide.

## Why Translation?

Every other Human Edge capability is about what you can do with AI. Translation is about what you can help *others* do.

Consider: AI companies spend billions building models, but adoption stalls because the people who need AI most — small business owners, nonprofit managers, community organizations, healthcare workers — cannot see how it applies to their actual problems. The bottleneck is not technology. The bottleneck is translation.

Translation is not "dumbing it down." It is the sophisticated cognitive work of:
- Understanding what your audience already knows (and doesn't)
- Finding the right analogies, examples, and entry points
- Designing exercises that produce genuine "aha" moments
- Adapting in real-time when your explanation isn't landing
- Measuring whether learning actually happened

This is the capability that makes every other capability valuable. If you can build a production AI system but cannot explain to a client why it matters, your system won't get deployed. If you can evaluate AI quality but cannot translate your evaluation into language a board understands, your judgment goes unheard.

Translation is what closes the loop between technical mastery and real-world impact.

## How the Studio Works: The Coaching Model

IS 482 — like every studio in the spine — operates on a coaching philosophy. Instructors are **coaches and entertainment directors** who create an environment where transformation happens through doing.

**The weekly rhythm (2 meetings/week):**

**Meeting 1 (e.g., Tuesday):**
1. **Canvas Quiz due before class** (auto-graded, 10-15 min) — covers last week's Magic Words and communication science concepts
2. **Story of the Week** — how Feynman explained quantum mechanics with zero jargon, how Sagan made billions of people care about astronomy, how Hopper made nanoseconds visible with a piece of wire. Not lectures — stories that give vocabulary.
3. **Studio work** — audience research, curriculum design, exercise prototyping, material development

**Meeting 2 (e.g., Thursday):**
1. **Peer critique session** — students present training materials, give and receive CRC feedback
2. **Translation workshop** — structured practice: explain this concept to someone with no technical background. Watch the confusion. Find the better analogy.
3. **Pass/fail participation** — showed up, engaged, gave thoughtful critique = pass

**Note on IS 482's unique rhythm:** Unlike earlier studios where Meeting 2 is peer critique of code or systems, IS 482's Meeting 2 often involves **live practice delivery** — students teach concepts to each other, playing the role of non-technical audience, and give feedback on clarity, cognitive load, and engagement. This is rehearsal for the live community event.

## Assessment

Assessment is designed to reward engagement and effort while reserving top grades for demonstrated excellence. The structure ensures that **consistent participation guarantees a passing grade**, while the A requires work that produces measurable community impact.

### Weekly Canvas Quizzes — 20%
- ~12 quizzes, auto-graded through Canvas
- Cover: cognitive science (cognitive load, working memory, curse of knowledge, transfer), pedagogical concepts (formative assessment, scaffolding, differentiation, Freire's banking vs. dialogue), communication techniques (analogies, metaphors, audience analysis, adaptive facilitation), security literacy (phishing, privacy, misinformation, verification), community engagement (needs assessment, cultural context, accessibility), impact measurement (pre/post design, qualitative coding, sample size)
- Lowest 2 scores dropped
- **Design intent:** Easy points for engaged students. They build the vocabulary of effective communication and teaching. If you do the reading and attend class, you'll score well.

### Studio Participation and Peer Critiques — 30%
- Graded **pass/fail per session** (~26 sessions)
- Criteria: present, engaged, gave CRC feedback on training materials, participated in practice delivery
- Up to 4 absences without penalty
- **Design intent:** Translation is a performance skill. You learn it by doing it, not by reading about it. The studio sessions are rehearsal space. Missing them means missing the practice that makes the live event work.

### Final Project + Showcase — 50%
- Community AI Training Event + Translation Brief + Community Impact Report, evaluated at Showcase (Week 15)
- **Scored by an instructor panel** (faculty + Center for Enterprise AI staff): each evaluator scores independently using a shared rubric, scores are aggregated
- Rubric categories:
  - **Event execution**: Did real community members attend, participate, and learn? Was facilitation adaptive?
  - **Materials quality**: Are they accessible, reusable, well-designed for non-technical audiences?
  - **Impact evidence**: Does the Community Impact Report show measurable change with honest analysis?
  - **Translation Brief depth**: Does the student demonstrate genuine understanding of the expert-to-public gap?
  - **Security literacy**: Was community security awareness addressed effectively?
- **Design intent:** This is where grade differentiation happens. A student who shows up every week, delivers a functional event, and writes a basic report earns a solid B. A student whose event produces measurable learning, whose materials are genuinely reusable, and whose Translation Brief reveals deep insight earns the A.

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

**How the math works:**
- All quizzes (18/20) + all sessions (30/30) + competent event + basic report (35/50) = **83 (B)**
- Aces quizzes (20/20) + all sessions (30/30) + outstanding event with measurable impact + deep Translation Brief (45/50) = **95 (A)**
- **The floor for an engaged student is a passing grade. The ceiling is earned.**

## Key Deliverables

### Community AI Training Event (live delivery)
A live, public training event delivered to a real community audience through the Center for Enterprise AI:

**Non-negotiable components:**
- **Real audience**: Community members, nonprofit staff, small business owners, or other non-technical participants — not classmates
- **Hands-on exercises**: Participants use AI tools to solve problems *they* care about during the session
- **Security literacy**: At least one session segment on protecting yourself in the age of AI (phishing, privacy, misinformation)
- **Adaptive facilitation**: Students must respond to real questions, confusion, and skepticism in real-time
- **Accessible design**: Materials must work for diverse skill levels, languages, and technological access
- **Ethical framing**: Honest discussion of AI limitations, risks, and responsible use — not hype

**Event standards:**
- Minimum 15 community participants (coordinated with Center for Enterprise AI)
- Pre-event survey to assess baseline AI knowledge
- Post-event survey to measure learning and confidence change
- Session recorded (with permission) for reflection and improvement
- All materials publicly available for future community use

### Training Curriculum and Materials
A complete, reusable training package:
- Workshop outline with learning objectives and timing
- Slide deck or visual aids designed for non-technical audiences (Hopper's nanosecond wire principle: make the abstract tangible)
- Hands-on exercise guides with step-by-step instructions
- Security awareness module
- Facilitator notes for future instructors
- Troubleshooting guide for common participant issues
- All materials tested with a pilot audience before the live event

### Translation Brief — Human Edge Artifact
A formal document reflecting on the translation process:
- What the audience knew before the training and what gaps existed
- What analogies, examples, and explanations worked — and which failed
- Where the "curse of knowledge" struck: moments where expertise made communication harder
- How the student adapted when their planned explanation didn't land
- Security topics: what was hardest to make accessible? What metaphors worked?
- Bret Victor's principle applied: where did making the invisible visible succeed? Where did it fail?
- How translation will inform the student's professional practice

This is the Translation artifact — the final Human Edge document in the program, closing the loop that began with Disciplined Inquiry in IS 117.

### Community Impact Report
An evidence-based assessment of the training's impact:
- Pre/post survey analysis showing measurable change in AI understanding and security awareness
- Participant quotes and qualitative feedback (coded and analyzed, not just listed)
- Assessment of which learning objectives were met and which need improvement
- Recommendations for future training iterations
- Honest analysis of what didn't work and why
- Connection to broader AI literacy goals for the Newark community
- Basic statistical analysis (confidence intervals where appropriate)

## The 40/60 Split in Practice

| Component | Hard-Way (40%) | Agentic (60%) |

The hard-way component ensures students truly understand their audience and make genuine human connections. The agentic component ensures they can produce professional-quality materials and scale their impact.
