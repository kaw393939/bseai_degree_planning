## Course Description

Students build their first website — a museum-style exhibit on a technology, person, or idea that changed computing — while learning to ask the questions that matter. The course teaches HTML, CSS, Git, the terminal, and design fundamentals, but the deeper curriculum is **how to inquire**: how to formulate a question worth asking, how to evaluate an answer (especially one generated by AI), and how to give and receive honest feedback.

The defining question: **"How do I know this is true?"**

Every student ships two projects: a curated museum website and a professional portfolio. Along the way, they learn that the most important skill in the age of AI is not knowing the answer — it's knowing what to ask.

## The Stories Behind the Technology

Great technologies don't appear out of nowhere. They are built by people with problems, convictions, and stories worth knowing. In IS 117, we meet the people whose work you'll use every day:

- **Tim Berners-Lee** wrote the first web browser and server at CERN in 1990 because physicists couldn't share documents across different computers. He gave the World Wide Web away for free. Every HTML page you build descends from that decision.
- **Linus Torvalds** created Git in 2005 because the Linux kernel community needed a version control system they could trust. He built the first version in ten days. When you type `git commit`, you're using a tool born from frustration and brilliant engineering.
- **Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn** designed TCP/IP — the protocol that makes the internet work — in the 1970s. They solved the problem of connecting networks that spoke different languages. Every time your browser loads a page, their architecture is underneath.
- **The first website** went live at CERN on August 6, 1991. It explained what the World Wide Web was. You can still visit it: info.cern.ch.

These stories are not trivia. They are the context that makes technology meaningful. When you understand *why* something was built, you understand *how* to use it well.

## Foundations: How Computers See the World

Before you build for the web, you need to understand what's happening underneath. IS 117 introduces foundational concepts that every computing professional should know — not at PhD depth, but well enough to ask the right questions and evaluate the answers:

- **Binary and bits**: How computers represent everything — numbers, text, images, instructions — as sequences of 0s and 1s. Why 8 bits make a byte. Why file sizes matter.
- **Character encoding (ASCII to UTF-8)**: How text goes from keystrokes to bytes and back again. Why "smart quotes" sometimes turn into garbage characters. Why UTF-8 won — and what Unicode means for a multilingual internet.
- **How the internet works**: DNS, HTTP, request/response cycles, client vs. server. When you type a URL, what actually happens — in enough detail to debug problems and explain it to a colleague.
- **How a browser renders a page**: HTML parsing, the DOM, CSS application, paint. Why your CSS sometimes doesn't do what you expect.

This is the "inch deep, mile wide" principle: you don't need to implement TCP/IP, but you need to know it exists, what it does, and when it matters.

## Giving and Receiving Feedback: The Studio Critique

A studio is not a lecture hall. In a studio, your work is visible, and your peers help you improve it.

IS 117 introduces **Toastmasters-style critique** as a core professional skill — because giving good feedback is as important as receiving it, and most people are terrible at both:

- **The CRC method**: Commend (what works), Recommend (what to improve), Commend (encouragement). Every critique follows this structure.
- **Specificity over vagueness**: "The color contrast on your navigation bar makes the links hard to read on mobile" is useful feedback. "It looks weird" is not.
- **Separating the work from the person**: You are critiquing a website, not a human being. This distinction is professional and must be practiced.
- **Receiving critique with grace**: Defensiveness kills learning. Students practice listening, asking clarifying questions, and saying "thank you" before deciding what to change.
- **Written feedback artifacts**: After each studio review, students write brief feedback notes for their peers. These become part of the studio culture.

This framework carries through all eight studios. By IS 425, students present to industry judges. The critique skills learned here make that possible.

## Why Disciplined Inquiry?

AI can answer any question in seconds. It cannot tell you whether the question was worth asking.

Disciplined Inquiry is the habit of pausing before accepting an answer — asking: where did this come from? What assumptions does it rest on? What would change my mind? In an age where AI generates fluent, confident, and sometimes wrong answers, the ability to interrogate rather than accept is the first Human Edge capability.

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Build** a multi-page website using semantic HTML and CSS (Create)
2. **Use** Git for version control with meaningful commits and branching (Apply)
3. **Navigate** the terminal environment confidently for file management and tool execution (Apply)
4. **Evaluate** AI-generated code and content for correctness, quality, and hidden assumptions (Evaluate)
5. **Formulate** precise questions that expose assumptions and lead to better answers (Analyze)
6. **Apply** visual design principles (contrast, hierarchy, typography, layout) to web interfaces (Apply)
7. **Give** structured, specific, and constructive feedback on peers' work using CRC critique (Apply)
8. **Explain** foundational computing concepts (binary, encoding, HTTP, DNS) in plain language (Understand)
9. **Construct** a Context Pack documenting what they know, don't know, and need to learn (Create)

## Assessment

Assessment in Studio 1 has two components, reflecting the program's belief that knowledge and judgment must develop together:

### AI-Generated Vocabulary Quizzes (30%)

Weekly quizzes covering terminology, concepts, and foundational knowledge — generated by AI and reviewed by the instructor. These are **knowledge verification**: can you define what a DNS record is, explain the difference between `margin` and `padding`, describe what binary encoding means?

- Approximately weekly, ~10 questions each
- Mix of multiple-choice, short-answer, and "explain in your own words"
- Cover both technical vocabulary and inquiry concepts from readings
- Students who engage with the material will pass easily; these catch disengagement early

### Qualitative Studio Reviews (70%)

The primary assessment is **in-studio, one-on-one or small-group review** between the instructor and student. These are essentially pass/fail checkpoints that document what the student can demonstrate and where they need to grow:

| Review Point | What's Evaluated |
|---|---|
| **Week 4 — Tools Review** | Terminal navigation, Git workflow, basic HTML/CSS. Can the student explain what their code does? |
| **Week 8 — Museum Site Review** | Multi-page site, design principles, semantic HTML. Can the student articulate design choices and respond to critique? |
| **Week 12 — Portfolio Review** | Professional portfolio, responsive design, accessibility basics. Can the student give CRC feedback on a peer's work? |
| **Week 15 — Final Studio Review** | Both projects complete, Context Pack, reflection on inquiry. Can the student evaluate AI-generated code and explain why they trust or distrust it? |

Each review is a **conversation, not an exam**. The instructor documents:
- What the student demonstrated
- What feedback was given
- What the student committed to improving
- Qualitative assessment: Exceeds / Meets / Approaching / Not Yet

The studio review model values **growth, honest self-assessment, and the quality of the student-teacher dialogue**. A student who struggles early but shows genuine improvement and honest reflection can earn a strong grade.

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90–100 | B+: 86–89 | B: 80–85 | C+: 76–79 | C: 70–75 | D: 60–69 | F: 0–59

*Final grades combine quiz scores (30%) and the instructor's holistic assessment across the four studio reviews (70%), with an emphasis on trajectory and demonstrated growth.*

## Key Deliverables

### Museum Website
A curated, multi-page website telling the story of a technology, person, or idea that changed computing. Students choose their subject (approved by instructor). Requirements:
- Minimum 4 pages with consistent navigation
- Semantic HTML throughout
- Responsive CSS (mobile-friendly)
- At least one page discussing "what could go wrong" — security implications, ethical concerns, or unintended consequences of the technology
- Cited sources (real links, not AI-generated citations)
- Deployed to GitHub Pages

### Professional Portfolio
A personal portfolio site that students will maintain throughout the program:
- About page, project showcase, contact information
- Responsive design with accessibility considerations
- Deployed and publicly accessible
- Becomes the container for all future studio projects

### Context Pack v1
A structured document capturing:
- What I know (and how I know it)
- What I don't know (and what I'd need to find out)
- Questions I've learned to ask
- How I evaluate AI-generated answers
