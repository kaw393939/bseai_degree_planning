## Course Description

Students build their first website — a museum-style exhibit on a technology, person, or idea that changed computing — while learning to ask the questions that matter. The course teaches HTML, CSS, Git, the terminal, and design fundamentals, but the deeper curriculum is **how to inquire**: how to formulate a question worth asking, how to evaluate an answer (especially one generated by AI), and how to give and receive honest feedback.

The defining question: **"How do I know this is true?"**

Every student ships two projects: a curated museum website and a professional portfolio. Along the way, they learn that the most important skill in the age of AI is not knowing the answer — it's knowing what to ask.

## Why Vocabulary Has Power

An LLM converts energy into intelligence — superhuman reading, writing, and deciding compressed into seconds. But the quality of that intelligence depends entirely on the context you provide. The same model that produces generic advice for a vague prompt produces expert-level analysis when you invoke the right names, concepts, and frameworks.

When you say "Tim Berners-Lee" to an AI, you don't just drop a name — you invoke the World Wide Web, CERN, open standards, the decision to give the web away for free, and decades of W3C governance. When you say "separation of concerns," you invoke the entire philosophy of clean architecture — HTML for structure, CSS for presentation, JavaScript for behavior. Each name and phrase is a **compressed knowledge packet** — a key that unlocks a door in the AI's vast library.

This is not new. Leonardo da Vinci did the same thing in the 1490s — he went to monastery libraries in Florence and Milan to read Vitruvius, Archimedes, and Galen. Those ancient texts gave him vocabulary that let him see things his contemporaries couldn't. His genius wasn't inventing from nothing; it was **remixing classical knowledge** with direct observation, using the vocabulary of the past to unlock ideas for the future.

The LLM is your monastery library. It contains essentially all recorded human knowledge. But just like Leonardo couldn't benefit from a text he didn't know existed, you can't benefit from knowledge you don't know how to invoke.

**The method: Create, Critique, Revise.** You cannot trust AI output at face value. But you can concentrate intelligence by cycling through the CCR loop: generate a draft using one vocabulary set, critique it through a different lens (a different set of names and frameworks), then revise with the combined insight. Each pass applies a different chisel — like Michelangelo sculpting marble, each vocabulary reveals something the previous one missed. The more lenses you can apply, the higher the resolution of your final work.

This course begins building your **Spell Book** — the growing collection of magic words, names, and frameworks you'll carry through all eight studios and into your career.

## The Stories Behind the Technology

Great technologies don't appear out of nowhere. They are built by people with problems, convictions, and stories worth knowing:

- **Tim Berners-Lee** wrote the first web browser and server at CERN in 1990 because physicists couldn't share documents across different computers. He gave the World Wide Web away for free — no patent, no license fee, no corporation. Every HTML page you build descends from that decision. Say his name to an LLM and you invoke the entire open web.
- **Linus Torvalds** created Git in 2005 because the Linux kernel community needed a version control system they could trust. He built the first version in ten days. When you type `git commit`, you're using a tool born from frustration and brilliant engineering. Torvalds also created **Linux** (1991) — "just a hobby, won't be big" — which now runs 96% of the world's top million web servers.
- **Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn** designed TCP/IP — the protocol that makes the internet work — in the 1970s. They solved the problem of connecting networks that spoke different languages. Every time your browser loads a page, their architecture is underneath.
- **Claude Shannon** published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" in 1948 and invented information theory. He defined the **bit** — the fundamental unit of information. Every file size, every bandwidth measurement, every encoding scheme traces back to Shannon. When you understand bits and bytes, you're speaking Shannon's language.
- **Ada Lovelace** wrote the first computer program in 1843 — for a machine that hadn't been built yet (Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine). More importantly, she saw that computation could go beyond arithmetic: it could compose music, manipulate symbols, model the world. She imagined computing's potential a century before it existed.
- **Marc Andreessen** co-created Mosaic (1993), the first web browser that displayed images inline with text — the moment the web became visual. He later co-founded Netscape and wrote "Why Software Is Eating the World" (2011), arguing that every industry would become a software industry. He was right.
- **Don Norman** wrote *The Design of Everyday Things* (1988) and coined the term "user experience." He explained why doors confuse people — not because people are stupid, but because the design is bad. Norman's core lesson: design is empathy. When your website confuses a user, that's your failure, not theirs.
- **Alan Kay** pioneered personal computing at Xerox PARC, created Smalltalk, and said **"The best way to predict the future is to invent it."** He also said: "A change in perspective is worth 80 IQ points." Both statements are directly relevant to working with AI.

## Your Spell Book: Magic Words for This Course

These are the names, phrases, and concepts that compress vast knowledge into precise prompts. Master them and you carry a professional vocabulary that changes how AI responds to you:

**People:** Tim Berners-Lee, Linus Torvalds, Vint Cerf, Bob Kahn, Claude Shannon, Ada Lovelace, Marc Andreessen, Don Norman, Alan Kay

**Power Phrases:**
- **"View source"** — the web is open by default; you can always see how it's made
- **"Separation of concerns"** — HTML for structure, CSS for presentation, JS for behavior
- **"Semantic markup"** — meaning over appearance; accessibility starts here
- **"First principles"** — reasoning from fundamentals, not from analogy
- **"Version control"** — track everything; every decision is reversible
- **"DRY" (Don't Repeat Yourself)** — the first rule of clean code
- **"CRC critique"** — Commend, Recommend, Commend — the feedback protocol for all 8 studios
- **"Progressive enhancement"** — build for the simplest case first, then layer complexity
- **"User experience"** — Norman's term; the whole interaction, not just the interface

Each phrase unlocks a conversation with the AI that's categorically richer than "make my website better."

## Foundations: How Computers See the World

Before you build for the web, you need to understand what's happening underneath:

- **Binary and bits**: How computers represent everything — numbers, text, images, instructions — as sequences of 0s and 1s. Shannon's bit is the atomic unit. Why 8 bits make a byte. Why file sizes matter.
- **Character encoding (ASCII to UTF-8)**: How text goes from keystrokes to bytes and back again. Why "smart quotes" sometimes turn into garbage characters. Why UTF-8 won — and what Unicode means for a multilingual internet.
- **How the internet works**: DNS, HTTP, request/response cycles, client vs. server. When you type a URL, what actually happens — Cerf and Kahn's TCP/IP is underneath everything.
- **How a browser renders a page**: HTML parsing, the DOM, CSS application, paint. Why your CSS sometimes doesn't do what you expect.

## Giving and Receiving Feedback: The Studio Critique

A studio is not a lecture hall. In a studio, your work is visible, and your peers help you improve it.

IS 117 introduces **Toastmasters-style critique** as a core professional skill:

- **The CRC method**: Commend (what works), Recommend (what to improve), Commend (encouragement). Every critique follows this structure.
- **Specificity over vagueness**: "The color contrast on your navigation bar makes the links hard to read on mobile" is useful. "It looks weird" is not.
- **Separating the work from the person**: You are critiquing a website, not a human being.
- **Receiving critique with grace**: Defensiveness kills learning. Students practice listening, asking clarifying questions, and saying "thank you" before deciding what to change.
- **Written feedback artifacts**: After each critique session, students write brief feedback notes for their peers.

This framework carries through all eight studios. By IS 425, students present to industry judges. The critique skills learned here make that possible.

## Why Disciplined Inquiry?

AI can answer any question in seconds. It cannot tell you whether the question was worth asking.

Disciplined Inquiry is the habit of pausing before accepting an answer — asking: where did this come from? What assumptions does it rest on? What would change my mind? In an age where AI generates fluent, confident, and sometimes wrong answers, the ability to interrogate rather than accept is the first Human Edge capability.

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Build** a multi-page website using semantic HTML and CSS (Create)
2. **Use** Git for version control with meaningful commits and branching (Apply)
3. **Navigate** the terminal environment confidently for file management and tool execution (Apply)
4. **Evaluate** AI-generated code and content for correctness, quality, and hidden assumptions (Evaluate)
5. **Formulate** precise questions that expose assumptions and lead to better answers (Analyze)
6. **Apply** visual design principles (contrast, hierarchy, typography, layout) following Don Norman's empathy-first approach (Apply)
7. **Give** structured, specific, and constructive feedback on peers' work using CRC critique (Apply)
8. **Explain** foundational computing concepts (binary, encoding, HTTP, DNS) in plain language (Understand)
9. **Use** the Create-Critique-Revise loop to concentrate AI intelligence through iterative refinement (Apply)
10. **Construct** a Context Pack documenting what they know, don't know, and need to learn (Create)

## How the Studio Works: The Coaching Model

IS 117 — like every studio in the spine — operates on a coaching philosophy, not a lecture-and-exam model. Instructors are **coaches and entertainment directors** whose job is to meet students where they are, treat them as whole people, and create an environment where transformation happens through doing.

**The weekly rhythm (2 meetings/week):**

**Meeting 1 (e.g., Tuesday):**
1. **Canvas Quiz due before class** (auto-graded, 10-15 min) — covers last week's Magic Words and concepts
2. **Story of the Week** — the instructor tells the origin story of a person, company, or concept (10-15 min). This is not a lecture — it's a story that gives students vocabulary they'll use all week.
3. **Studio work** — hands on keyboards, building the project, applying concepts. Instructor circulates, coaches, troubleshoots.

**Meeting 2 (e.g., Thursday):**
1. **Peer critique session** — students present progress, give and receive CRC feedback
2. **Round-robin check-ins** — with 20-25 students, everyone gets heard
3. **Pass/fail participation** — you showed up, you engaged, you gave thoughtful critique = pass

**The design intent:** Passing is achievable for anyone who shows up and engages. The quizzes reward consistency. The critiques reward presence. The distinction between good and great happens at Demo Day, where the instructor panel scores work quantitatively. The studio is a gym, not a filter. The instructor is the coach who pushes you further than you'd push yourself — but who also spots you when the weight gets heavy.

## Assessment

Assessment rewards engagement and effort while reserving top grades for demonstrated excellence. **Consistent participation guarantees a passing grade**; the A requires work that impresses a panel of faculty evaluators.

### Weekly Canvas Quizzes — 20%
- ~12 quizzes across the semester, auto-graded through Canvas
- Cover vocabulary, concepts, and foundational knowledge: HTML elements, CSS properties, Git commands, Shannon's bits, Norman's design principles, Berners-Lee's web architecture
- Multiple choice, matching, short answer — objective and auto-graded
- Lowest 2 scores dropped
- **Design intent:** Easy points for engaged students that build professional vocabulary.

### Studio Participation and Peer Critiques — 30%
- Graded **pass/fail per session** (~26 sessions across 2 meetings/week)
- Criteria: present, engaged, gave CRC feedback to peers, participated in discussion
- Documented by the instructor at each session
- Up to 4 absences without penalty
- **Design intent:** Gets people into the room, talking to each other, building the social climate that makes a studio work.

### Final Project + Demo Day — 50%
- Museum website + professional portfolio, evaluated at Demo Day (Week 15)
- **Scored by an instructor panel**: each evaluator scores independently using a shared rubric, scores are aggregated
- Rubric categories:
  - **Technical quality**: Semantic HTML, clean CSS, responsive design, Git history
  - **Inquiry evidence**: Quality of research, source citations, questioning depth
  - **Design quality**: Norman's principles applied — contrast, hierarchy, typography, layout
  - **Defense quality**: Can the student explain every decision? Do they handle questions with honesty?
  - **Spell Book demonstration**: Can the student use Magic Words to enhance AI-assisted work?
- **Design intent:** A student who shows up every week and builds a functional site earns a solid B. A student whose work is well-designed, deeply researched, and confidently defended earns the A.

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

**How the math works:**
- A student who completes all quizzes (18/20), attends all sessions (30/30), and builds a competent project (35/50) earns an **83 (B)** — solid effort, solid grade.
- A student who aces quizzes (20/20), attends all sessions (30/30), and delivers outstanding work scored highly by the panel (45/50) earns a **95 (A)**.
- A student who does the quizzes (15/20) and attends most sessions (25/30) but delivers a minimal project (25/50) earns a **65 (D)**.
- **The floor for an engaged student is a passing grade. The ceiling is earned through excellent work.**

## Key Deliverables

### Museum Website
A curated, multi-page website telling the story of a technology, person, or idea that changed computing:
- Minimum 4 pages with consistent navigation
- Semantic HTML throughout
- Responsive CSS (mobile-friendly)
- At least one page discussing "what could go wrong" — security implications, ethical concerns, or unintended consequences
- Cited sources (real links, not AI-generated citations)
- Deployed to GitHub Pages
- Evidence of the CCR loop: show how you used Create-Critique-Revise with AI to improve the work

### Professional Portfolio
A personal portfolio site maintained throughout the program:
- About page, project showcase, contact information
- Responsive design with accessibility considerations
- Deployed and publicly accessible
- Becomes the container for all future studio projects

### Context Pack v1
A structured document capturing:
- What I know (and how I know it)
- What I don't know (and what I'd need to find out)
- My Spell Book so far — magic words I've learned and how they change AI output
- Questions I've learned to ask
- How I evaluate AI-generated answers
