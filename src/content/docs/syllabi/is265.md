# IS 265 — Problem Analysis in IS

**Credits:** 3 | **Contact hours:** 3 hrs/week | **Mode:** Studio (face-to-face)
**Human Edge capability:** Problem Finding
**40/60 split:** Manual stakeholder interviews and analysis / AI-directed research and documentation
**Prerequisites:** IS 218

<Aside type="tip" title="Position in the spine">
This is **Studio 4** — the midpoint of the spine. Students shift from building technology to understanding the human and business problems technology is supposed to solve. This is where engineers become problem-finders.
</Aside>

---

## Course Description

Students learn that the hardest part of building software is not writing the code — it's figuring out what to build and for whom. Through real stakeholder interviews, process mapping, requirements elicitation, and business case analysis, students develop the discipline of **Problem Finding**: the ability to see the real problem hiding behind the stated one.

The defining question: **"Is this actually the right problem to solve?"**

Most failed projects don't fail because of bad code. They fail because someone built an excellent solution to the wrong problem. IS 265 trains students to resist the rush to solution and instead invest in understanding the problem deeply enough to know whether it's worth solving at all.

## The Stories Behind the Companies

Every great technology company started with someone who saw a problem nobody else was solving — or solved it in a way nobody expected:

- **Jeff Bezos** left a hedge fund job in 1994 to sell books online from his garage. Not because he loved books — because he'd seen a statistic: web usage was growing 2,300% per year, and books were the perfect product for online retail (standardized, huge selection, easy to ship). Amazon's origin story is a lesson in **problem finding through data**: Bezos didn't ask "what's a cool thing to build?" He asked "what does the data say people will want?" Today Amazon is a $2 trillion company. It started with a spreadsheet.
- **Pierre Omidyar** launched eBay in 1995 — supposedly to help his fiancee trade Pez dispensers, though that's a PR myth. The real insight was deeper: millions of people had things to sell and no efficient way to find buyers. eBay solved the **matching problem** at scale. The first item sold? A broken laser pointer, for $14.83. Omidyar confirmed with the buyer: "You know it's broken, right?" "I'm a collector of broken laser pointers." The lesson: there's a market for everything if you can connect supply and demand.
- **The PayPal Mafia** — Peter Thiel, Max Levchin, Elon Musk, Reid Hoffman, and others — built PayPal because sending money online was impossible in 1999. Banks wouldn't do it. The team pivoted multiple times (from cryptography to Palm Pilot payments to email money) before finding the product that worked. After eBay acquired PayPal, the founders scattered and built LinkedIn, YouTube, Tesla, SpaceX, Yelp, and Palantir. The lesson: **pivoting is not failure — it's Problem Finding in action.**
- **Fred Brooks** managed the IBM System/360 project in the 1960s — one of the largest software projects in history. It was late, over budget, and nearly broke IBM. Brooks distilled what he learned into *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975), including the most famous law in software engineering: **"Adding people to a late project makes it later."** And the most important essay: **"No Silver Bullet"** — there is no single technique that will make software development ten times better. The lesson: understanding the nature of the problem (essential vs. accidental complexity) is more important than any tool.

These are not bedtime stories. They are **case studies in Problem Finding** — the skill this course teaches.

## The Entrepreneurial Mindset

IS 265 is where entrepreneurial thinking moves from introduction (IS 118) to deep practice:

- **The Lean Startup** (Eric Ries, 2011): Build-Measure-Learn. The minimum viable experiment. Validated learning over vanity metrics. Students apply these concepts to their stakeholder projects: before building anything, what's the cheapest way to test whether this problem is real and this solution is wanted?
- **Business Model Canvas** (Alexander Osterwalder): A one-page framework that forces you to think about value proposition, customer segments, channels, revenue streams, cost structure, and key partnerships. Students create canvases for their project proposals.
- **The Mythical Man-Month** applied: Brooks's distinction between essential complexity (inherent in the problem) and accidental complexity (introduced by the solution) is the most powerful lens in Problem Finding. Before you propose a solution, ask: how much of the difficulty is in the problem itself, and how much is in the way we're approaching it?
- **Robert Cialdini's** *Influence* (already in the curriculum): six principles of persuasion (reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity) — because finding the right problem is not enough. You must persuade stakeholders that it's worth solving.
- **Jobs to Be Done** (Clayton Christensen): People don't buy products. They hire them to do a job. "What job is this software being hired to do?" is the most powerful question in Problem Finding.

## Foundations: The Mathematics of Business Decisions

Problem Analysis requires quantitative reasoning. IS 265 builds business math through real project work:

- **ROI and payback period**: If the proposed system costs $50K to build and saves $2K/month in labor, when does it pay for itself? Is that fast enough to justify the risk? This is arithmetic, but the judgment required to estimate the inputs is the hard part.
- **Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)**: Building the system is chapter one. Hosting, maintaining, updating, training users, handling support tickets — that's the rest of the book. Students learn to estimate ongoing costs, not just development costs.
- **Net Present Value basics**: A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. When comparing projects with different timelines, you need to discount future cash flows. This is not deep finance — but it's the minimum numeracy required to write a credible business case.
- **Estimation and Fermi problems**: How many workshop registrations will the Center handle per month? How much data will that generate? How many support tickets? Students practice order-of-magnitude estimation — the skill that separates "I don't know" from "my best estimate, with stated assumptions, is..."

## Why Problem Finding?

AI can analyze data, summarize documents, and generate requirements from transcripts. It cannot sit across from a frustrated department head, notice that the stated problem ("we need a better dashboard") hides the real problem ("nobody trusts the data"), and reframe the conversation.

Problem Finding is the ability to see the problem behind the problem — to recognize when stakeholders are solving the wrong thing, when requirements conflict with reality, when the elegant solution misses the point. This requires empathy, skepticism, and the courage to say "I think we're solving the wrong problem."

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Conduct** stakeholder interviews that uncover needs, constraints, and unstated assumptions (Apply)
2. **Map** business processes identifying bottlenecks, waste, and improvement opportunities (Analyze)
3. **Write** requirements documents distinguishing stated needs from real needs (Create)
4. **Create** business cases with ROI analysis, risk assessment, and stakeholder mapping (Create)
5. **Apply** Lean Startup principles to validate problem hypotheses before committing to solutions (Apply)
6. **Analyze** how AI components change problem dynamics — new capabilities, new risks, new stakeholder concerns (Analyze)
7. **Persuade** stakeholders using evidence and structured argumentation (Cialdini principles applied ethically) (Apply)
8. **Maintain** an Assumptions Log documenting what you believe to be true and how each assumption could be tested or falsified (Create)
9. **Construct** a Context Pack documenting problem context, stakeholder landscape, and analytical reasoning (Create)

## Assessment

### AI-Generated Vocabulary Quizzes (30%)

Weekly quizzes covering business analysis, entrepreneurial, and problem-finding concepts:

- Requirements engineering (functional vs. non-functional, user stories, acceptance criteria)
- Business case concepts (ROI, TCO, NPV, payback period, risk assessment)
- Lean and Agile terminology (MVP, pivot, validated learning, sprint, retrospective)
- Stakeholder analysis (power/interest grid, influence mapping)
- Cialdini's persuasion principles and ethical application
- Brooks's concepts (essential vs. accidental complexity, second-system effect, surgical team)
- Company origin stories and the Problem Finding lessons they illustrate

### Qualitative Studio Reviews (70%)

| Review Point | What's Evaluated |
|---|---|
| **Week 4 — Stakeholder Analysis Review** | Stakeholder map, interview transcripts, initial needs analysis. Can the student distinguish stated needs from real needs? Can they explain the Bezos/eBay origin stories as examples of Problem Finding? |
| **Week 8 — Midterm: Problem Definition + Business Case** | Problem statement, process map, business case with ROI. Can the student argue that this is the right problem to solve? Does the business case use honest numbers with stated assumptions? |
| **Week 12 — Requirements + Prototype Review** | Requirements document, Business Model Canvas, low-fidelity prototype. Can the student show evidence of validated learning — did they test assumptions before committing to a solution? |
| **Week 15 — Final Studio Review: Problem Analysis Package** | Complete analysis package, Assumptions Log, persuasive presentation. Can the student defend their analysis to skeptical reviewers? Can they identify what they got wrong and how they discovered it? |

Each review documents: what the student demonstrated, feedback given, commitments for improvement, qualitative rating (Exceeds / Meets / Approaching / Not Yet).

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90–100 | B+: 86–89 | B: 80–85 | C+: 76–79 | C: 70–75 | D: 60–69 | F: 0–59

*Final grades combine quiz scores (30%) and the instructor's holistic assessment across the four studio reviews (70%).*

## Signature Assessment: The Assumptions Log

The Assumptions Log is IS 265's distinctive artifact. Throughout the semester, students maintain a living document:

| Assumption | Source | Confidence | Test | Result |
|------------|--------|------------|------|--------|
| Workshops have 15-30 attendees | Center staff interview | Medium | Check registration data for last 8 events | Actual: 8-45, bimodal distribution. Assumption wrong. |
| Users prefer email confirmations | "Everyone uses email" | Low | Survey 20 potential users | 60% prefer text. Update requirements. |

The Assumptions Log teaches the most important Problem Finding skill: **knowing what you don't know, and having a plan to find out.** It carries forward into every subsequent studio.

## Key Deliverables

### Problem Analysis Package (final project)
A comprehensive analysis for a real organizational problem (sourced from the Center for Enterprise AI or industry partners):
- **Stakeholder Map**: who cares, who decides, who's affected, who's ignored
- **Process Map**: current state workflow with bottlenecks and waste identified
- **Problem Statement**: the real problem (not just the stated one), with evidence
- **Business Case**: ROI analysis, TCO estimate, risk assessment, payback timeline
- **Business Model Canvas**: value proposition, segments, channels, revenue, costs
- **Requirements Document**: functional and non-functional, with traceability to stakeholder needs
- **Low-fidelity Prototype**: enough to test key assumptions with real users
- **Assumptions Log**: complete record of beliefs, tests, and results
- **Presentation**: persuasive defense of the analysis to a review panel

### Security Considerations
Every Problem Analysis Package must include:
- **Data security assessment**: what sensitive data does this system handle? What are the risks?
- **Access control requirements**: who should see what? How is authorization managed?
- **Compliance landscape**: are there regulations (FERPA, HIPAA, PCI-DSS) that apply?
- **AI-specific risks**: if AI components are proposed, what are the privacy, bias, and accountability implications?
