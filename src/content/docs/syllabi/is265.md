# IS 265 — Problem Analysis in IS

**Credits:** 3 | **Contact hours:** 2 meetings/week | **Mode:** Studio (face-to-face, 20-25 students)
**Human Edge capability:** Problem Finding
**40/60 split:** Manual stakeholder interviews and analysis / AI-directed research and documentation
**Prerequisites:** IS 218

<Aside type="tip" title="Position in the spine">
This is **Studio 4** — the midpoint of the spine. Students shift from building technology to understanding the human and business problems technology is supposed to solve. This is where engineers become problem-finders.
</Aside>

---

## Course Description

Students learn that the hardest part of building software is not writing the code — it's figuring out what to build and for whom. Through real stakeholder interviews, process mapping, requirements elicitation, and business case analysis, students develop the discipline of **Problem Finding**: the ability to see the real problem hiding behind the stated one.

The defining question: **"Is this actually the right problem to solve?"**

Most failed projects don't fail because of bad code. They fail because someone built an excellent solution to the wrong problem. IS 265 trains students to resist the rush to solution and instead invest in understanding the problem deeply enough to know whether it's worth solving at all.

## Why Vocabulary Has Power

An LLM converts energy into intelligence — superhuman reading, writing, and deciding compressed into seconds. But the quality of that intelligence depends entirely on the vocabulary you bring.

When you say "Jobs to Be Done" to an AI, you don't just invoke a framework — you invoke Clayton Christensen, Harvard Business School, the milkshake study, and an entire theory of why products succeed or fail. When you say "5 Whys," you invoke Sakichi Toyoda, the Toyota Production System, root cause analysis, and the discipline of digging past symptoms to causes. When you say "essential vs. accidental complexity," you invoke Fred Brooks, IBM System/360, *The Mythical Man-Month*, and the deepest insight in software engineering: that no tool can eliminate the complexity inherent in the problem itself.

Leonardo read Vitruvius before designing buildings. You invoke Brooks before analyzing systems. The principle is identical: history gives you vocabulary, vocabulary gives you precision, and precision changes what the AI can do for you.

**The method: Create, Critique, Revise.** In IS 265, the CCR loop is applied to business analysis. Create a problem statement with AI assistance. Critique it using Brooks's essential/accidental distinction. Revise. Critique again using Christensen's Jobs to Be Done. Revise again. Each vocabulary lens reveals a different aspect of the problem. The student with five lenses sees what the student with one lens misses.

## The Stories Behind the Companies

Every great technology company started with someone who saw a problem nobody else was solving:

- **Jeff Bezos** left a hedge fund job in 1994 to sell books online from his garage. Not because he loved books — because he'd seen a statistic: web usage was growing 2,300% per year, and books were the perfect product for online retail (standardized, huge selection, easy to ship). Amazon started with a spreadsheet. The lesson: **problem finding through data**.
- **Pierre Omidyar** launched eBay in 1995 — supposedly to help his fiancee trade Pez dispensers, though that's a PR myth. The real insight: millions of people had things to sell and no efficient way to find buyers. The first item sold? A broken laser pointer, for $14.83. Omidyar confirmed: "You know it's broken, right?" "I'm a collector of broken laser pointers." The lesson: there's a market for everything if you solve the **matching problem**.
- **The PayPal Mafia** — Peter Thiel, Max Levchin, Elon Musk, Reid Hoffman — built PayPal because sending money online was impossible in 1999. They pivoted multiple times before finding the product that worked. After eBay acquired PayPal, they built LinkedIn, YouTube, Tesla, SpaceX, Yelp, and Palantir. The lesson: **pivoting is Problem Finding in action.**
- **Fred Brooks** managed the IBM System/360 in the 1960s — one of the largest software projects in history. Late, over budget, nearly broke IBM. He distilled what he learned into *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975): **"Adding people to a late project makes it later."** And the most important essay in software: **"No Silver Bullet"** — essential vs. accidental complexity. Understanding the nature of the problem matters more than any tool.
- **Sakichi Toyoda** developed the **5 Whys** technique at Toyota in the 1930s as part of the Toyota Production System. When a machine stopped, he asked "why?" five times until he reached the root cause. This simple technique is the most powerful debugging tool in business: it forces you past symptoms to causes. Say "5 Whys" to an LLM and it immediately structures a root cause analysis.
- **Steve Blank** created the **Customer Development** methodology (2005), arguing that startups fail not because they can't build products, but because they build products nobody wants. His four-step process — Customer Discovery, Customer Validation, Customer Creation, Company Building — is the systematic version of Problem Finding. Blank's insight: **"Get out of the building."** You can't find problems sitting at your desk.
- **Clayton Christensen** (Harvard Business School) developed the **Jobs to Be Done** framework: people don't buy products, they hire them to do a job. His famous example: people don't buy a quarter-inch drill because they want a drill — they want a quarter-inch hole. Say "Jobs to Be Done" to an AI and it restructures any product analysis around customer intent.

- **"Reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity"** — Cialdini's six principles of persuasion
- **"Fermi estimation"** — order-of-magnitude reasoning; "I don't know" becomes "my best estimate is..."

## Foundations: The Mathematics of Business Decisions

Problem Analysis requires quantitative reasoning:

- **ROI and payback period**: If the proposed system costs $50K to build and saves $2K/month, when does it pay for itself? The judgment to estimate the inputs is the hard part.
- **Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)**: Building is chapter one. Hosting, maintaining, updating, training users, support tickets — that's the rest of the book.
- **Net Present Value basics**: A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. Minimum numeracy for a credible business case.
- **Estimation and Fermi problems**: How many workshop registrations per month? How much data? How many support tickets? The skill that separates "I don't know" from "my best estimate, with stated assumptions, is..." Enrico Fermi could estimate the number of piano tuners in Chicago. You can estimate the load on your system.

## The Entrepreneurial Mindset

IS 265 is where entrepreneurial thinking moves from introduction (IS 118) to deep practice:

- **The Lean Startup** (Ries): Build-Measure-Learn, MVP, validated learning vs. vanity metrics
- **Business Model Canvas** (Osterwalder): Value proposition, customer segments, channels, revenue, costs
- **Customer Development** (Blank): Get out of the building. Find the problem before you build the solution.
- **The Mythical Man-Month** applied: Brooks's essential/accidental distinction is the lens for every problem
- **Cialdini's six principles**: Reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity — because finding the right problem is not enough. You must persuade stakeholders it's worth solving.

## Why Problem Finding?

AI can analyze data, summarize documents, and generate requirements. It cannot sit across from a frustrated department head, notice that "we need a better dashboard" hides "nobody trusts the data," and reframe the conversation.

Problem Finding is the ability to see the problem behind the problem — to recognize when stakeholders are solving the wrong thing, when requirements conflict with reality, when the elegant solution misses the point.

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Conduct** stakeholder interviews that uncover needs, constraints, and unstated assumptions (Apply)
2. **Map** business processes identifying bottlenecks, waste, and improvement opportunities (Analyze)
3. **Write** requirements documents distinguishing stated needs from real needs (Create)
4. **Create** business cases with ROI, TCO, risk assessment, and stakeholder mapping (Create)
5. **Apply** Lean Startup and Customer Development to validate problem hypotheses before committing to solutions (Apply)
6. **Analyze** how AI components change problem dynamics — new capabilities, new risks, new concerns (Analyze)
7. **Persuade** stakeholders using Cialdini's principles applied ethically (Apply)
8. **Maintain** an Assumptions Log tracking beliefs, confidence levels, tests, and results (Create)
9. **Apply** the CCR loop to business analysis: create-with-one-framework, critique-with-another, revise (Apply)
10. **Construct** a Context Pack documenting problem context, stakeholder landscape, and analytical reasoning (Create)

## How the Studio Works: The Coaching Model

IS 265 — like every studio in the spine — operates on a coaching philosophy. Instructors are **coaches and entertainment directors** who create an environment where transformation happens through doing.

**The weekly rhythm (2 meetings/week):**

**Meeting 1 (e.g., Tuesday):**
1. **Canvas Quiz due before class** (auto-graded, 10-15 min) — covers last week's Magic Words and concepts
2. **Story of the Week** — the instructor tells a company origin story or introduces a framework through its creator. How Bezos found the book problem. How the PayPal Mafia pivoted. How Brooks watched System/360 fail.
3. **Studio work** — stakeholder research, process mapping, requirements writing, business case analysis

**Meeting 2 (e.g., Thursday):**
1. **Peer critique session** — students present analysis, give and receive CRC feedback
2. **Stakeholder practice** — role-play interviews, presentation rehearsal, persuasion practice
3. **Pass/fail participation** — showed up, engaged, gave thoughtful critique = pass

## Assessment

Assessment rewards engagement while reserving top grades for demonstrated excellence. **Consistent participation guarantees a passing grade.**

### Weekly Canvas Quizzes — 20%
- ~12 quizzes, auto-graded through Canvas
- Cover: requirements engineering, business case concepts, Lean/Agile terminology, stakeholder analysis, Cialdini's principles, Brooks's concepts, company origin stories, Fermi estimation
- Lowest 2 scores dropped

### Studio Participation and Peer Critiques — 30%
- Graded **pass/fail per session** (~26 sessions)
- Up to 4 absences without penalty

### Final Project + Demo Day — 50%
- Problem Analysis Package, evaluated at Demo Day (Week 15)
- **Scored by an instructor panel** using a shared rubric:
  - **Problem definition quality**: Does the analysis distinguish stated from real needs? Is the 5 Whys/Jobs to Be Done work visible?
  - **Business case rigor**: ROI, TCO, risk — with honest stated assumptions, not made-up numbers
  - **Stakeholder evidence**: Real interviews, real insights, real pivots
  - **Defense quality**: Can the student handle "you're solving the wrong problem" from the panel?
  - **Spell Book application**: Can the student demonstrate how vocabulary (Brooks, Christensen, Cialdini) changed their analysis?

**Letter Grade Scale:** A: 90-100 | B+: 86-89 | B: 80-85 | C+: 76-79 | C: 70-75 | D: 60-69 | F: 0-59

**How the math works:**
- All quizzes (18/20) + all sessions (30/30) + competent analysis (35/50) = **83 (B)**
- Aces quizzes (20/20) + all sessions (30/30) + outstanding analysis defended brilliantly (45/50) = **95 (A)**
- **The floor for an engaged student is a passing grade. The ceiling is earned.**

## Signature Assessment: The Assumptions Log

Throughout the semester, students maintain a living document:

| Assumption | Source | Confidence | Test | Result |
|------------|--------|------------|------|--------|
| Workshops have 15-30 attendees | Center staff interview | Medium | Check registration data for last 8 events | Actual: 8-45, bimodal. Assumption wrong. |
| Users prefer email confirmations | "Everyone uses email" | Low | Survey 20 potential users | 60% prefer text. Update requirements. |

The Assumptions Log teaches the most important Problem Finding skill: **knowing what you don't know, and having a plan to find out.** This is the 5 Whys applied to your own beliefs.
