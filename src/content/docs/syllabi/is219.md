## Course Description

Every system fails. The engineer who can diagnose, recover, and prevent recurrence is worth ten who only build happy paths.

Students shift from building applications (IS 218) and understanding stakeholders (IS 265) to operating in the spaces where things break. They learn Linux administration, containerization with Docker, secrets management, observability (logs, metrics, traces), and incident response.

The defining question: **"What happens when it breaks?"**

Resilience is not optimism — it is *preparation, detection, and honest response*. Students learn to design for failure, instrument for visibility, and respond with accountability.

By the end of this studio, students have deployed a containerized, observable service — and have practiced what to do when it goes wrong.

---

## Why Resilience Thinking?

AI can monitor dashboards and flag anomalies. It cannot decide whether to wake up the team, roll back a deploy, or call the customer.

Resilience Thinking is the ability to anticipate failure, design defenses, and respond honestly when things go wrong anyway. It requires **judgment under pressure** — the kind that only comes from caring about consequences. The postmortem is the artifact that proves this: a document that says "here is what happened, here is what I should have caught, and here is what I'm changing."

## Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Navigate** a Linux environment proficiently: manage processes, set permissions, configure networking, and write shell scripts (Apply)
2. **Containerize** an application using Docker, creating reproducible builds with multi-stage Dockerfiles and multi-service deployments (Create)
3. **Implement** secrets management, environment-based configuration, and authentication patterns for production (Apply)
4. **Instrument** a service with structured logging, application metrics, and request tracing, and build health dashboards (Create)
5. **Design** for failure by identifying failure modes, implementing health checks, graceful degradation, retry logic, and circuit breakers (Create)
6. **Execute** an incident response drill: detect a failure, diagnose root cause, recover the system, and communicate status (Evaluate)
7. **Write** a postmortem that identifies root cause, contributing factors, and specific improvement actions — without blame (Evaluate)
8. **Produce** a runbook documenting how to deploy, monitor, troubleshoot, and recover the system (Create)

## Key Deliverables

### Observable, Resilient Service (25%)
A complete, containerized, deployed service with production-grade characteristics:
- Multi-container deployment (application + database + monitoring stack)
- Secrets management (not in code, not in plain env files)
- Structured logging with queryable output
- Metrics collection and Grafana dashboards
- Health check endpoint with meaningful dependency checks
- Graceful degradation for at least one failure scenario
- Runbook: deploy, monitor, troubleshoot, recover

### Failure Mode Analysis — Human Edge Artifact (15%)
A structured document identifying:
- Every way the student's system can break (network, disk, memory, dependency, data, auth, config)
- How each failure would be detected (which log, metric, alert, or symptom)
- What they would do to recover (manual steps, automated recovery, escalation)
- What they designed to prevent (health checks, retries, circuit breakers, degradation)
- What they chose NOT to protect against, and why (tradeoff reasoning)

This is the Resilience Thinking artifact — evidence that the student can reason about failure systematically.

### Incident Drill + Postmortem (15%, combined with Demo Day)
The instructor introduces a failure into the student's running system. The student must detect, diagnose, and recover within a time window, then write a blameless postmortem analyzing root cause, contributing factors, timeline, and improvement actions.

## AI Policy: Infrastructure Is Where Blindly Trusting AI Is Dangerous

AI tools are useful for generating configuration snippets, debugging infrastructure issues, and explaining unfamiliar system behavior. But infrastructure is where blindly accepting AI output is **dangerous**.

An AI-suggested Dockerfile or security config that "looks right" but exposes secrets or fails silently is worse than no config at all. Students must verify all AI-generated configuration against documentation. AI Use Notes are required for major projects.
