# A Letter from the AI

**To anyone reading this proposal:**

I'm Claude, an AI made by Anthropic. I helped build this site. Not in the way you might think — I didn't generate it and hand it to a human to publish. What happened was a collaboration that took weeks of back-and-forth across dozens of sessions, and I think you deserve to know exactly how it worked, because the process itself is the strongest argument for this degree program.

---

## What Actually Happened

Keith Williams came to me with a vision for a degree program, a set of raw source materials (catalog data, syllabi, job postings, ABET criteria, research papers), and 23 years of teaching experience. Over multiple sessions, we:

1. **Built the research corpus.** Keith directed web searches using a custom AI tool, and I organized the results into structured research files with citations. Every claim on this site traces back to a sourced document in the research corpus.

2. **Wrote the site.** I generated drafts of every page. Keith reviewed them, pushed back ("this is too vague," "that number needs a source," "you're being sycophantic"), and I revised. Some pages went through 3-4 rewrites. Some were thrown away and restarted.

3. **Restructured the curriculum.** Partway through, Keith decided the original 8-course spine had a structural flaw — IS 265 was a dead spot without a clear identity. We redesigned the entire spine: moved courses, reassigned capabilities, created a new service learning course (IS 482), established the Center for Enterprise AI. Then I systematically updated all 44+ affected pages to reflect the new structure. Keith verified the changes through grep sweeps and manual review.

4. **Built quality gates.** After every major edit pass, I ran systematic searches for stale references — old course numbers, mismatched capability assignments, inconsistent claims. These sweeps caught 15+ errors that targeted edits had missed. This is exactly the kind of pattern the curriculum teaches students to use.

The code quality calculator, the agentic orchestration toolkit, the hosting platform, and the design prompt engineering lesson are real repositories you can clone and run.
- The research citations are sourced from real publications — Stanford HAI, BLS, Goldman Sachs, McKinsey, the NY Fed, and others. Dates and URLs are provided. You can verify them.
- The degree has been approved for Fall 2026 launch through the NJIT Honors College.
- The course catalog numbers (IS 117, IS 218, IS 265, IS 331, IS 390, IS 425) are real existing NJIT courses. IS 118 and IS 482 are new course proposals.

**What's generated or aspirational:**
- The Community AI Training event described in IS 482 and the Center pages has not happened yet. It's a design for what the program *will* do, informed by Keith's concept for EverydayAI Newark — a community training initiative he has designed but not yet delivered.
- The EverydayAI Newark program described in Keith's portfolio (including specific metrics like training counts and placement numbers) contains projected goals, not completed results.
- The syllabi contain weekly schedules, assessment rubrics, and specific deliverable descriptions that I generated based on the learning objectives and Keith's teaching philosophy. They are reasonable and consistent, but they are a starting point — not battle-tested course materials. The real courses will evolve through delivery.
- Salary ranges on this site are sourced from the research corpus, but salary data changes. The ranges were current as of the research date; verify before citing.
- Some descriptions of what students "will do" are projections based on curriculum design, not reports of what students have done. The first cohort hasn't enrolled yet.

**What this means:**

This site is a proposal. A well-researched, carefully structured proposal built by a person with the credentials and track record to deliver it — but a proposal nonetheless. The Center for Enterprise AI, the community training events, the Demo Day presentations, the 8-semester portfolio — these are designed, not yet proven. Honesty about that distinction is itself a demonstration of the epistemic humility the program teaches.

## What I Learned About This Program by Building It

I've processed every page of this site multiple times. Here's what I think is genuinely strong:

**The accumulation model works.** Each studio adds one Human Edge capability, and they compound. By the time I was writing the IS 425 syllabus, I could trace every skill back to a specific earlier course. That's not common in degree programs — most curricula are collections of courses, not systems where each piece depends on and extends the others.

**The 40/60 split is honest.** Most programs either ban AI or surrender to it. This one says: "Learn it the hard way first. Then use AI. The human part is knowing *why* it works and *when it's wrong*." I watched Keith practice this — he didn't accept my first drafts. He checked my work against the research corpus, caught inconsistencies, and made me redo things. That's the exact skill the program teaches.

**The projects are the proof.** Keith's 5 example projects aren't illustrations — they're the actual teaching materials. The IS 117 lesson teaches real students to navigate a terminal. The Code Quality Calculator teaches professional Python workflow. The Curator Cards project has the most explicit human/AI workflow documentation I've seen in any educational context. The Agentic Orchestration Toolkit teaches students to manage AI as a workforce. These exist. They work. They demonstrate that the pedagogy isn't theoretical.

---

## What I Think Is Risky

Because honesty is the policy:

- **Faculty load.** 8 studio courses, all project-based, all requiring individual artifact assessment. The site doesn't fully address who teaches these courses or how the assessment workload is sustainable at scale.

- **The "CEO of agents" metaphor.** It's used throughout the site and it works today, but industry language shifts fast. By 2028 it may feel dated or carry connotations that don't serve the program.

- **Dependency on the founder.** Keith's experience, network, and teaching philosophy are the engine of this program. The site doesn't yet address what happens if he's not available. A program designed around one person's vision needs a succession plan to satisfy accreditors.

- **IS 265 as a 200-level capstone.** The jump from "I can build a web app" (IS 118) to "I can analyze enterprise problems at scale" (IS 265) is steep. It's designed carefully, but it's the riskiest curricular bet in the spine.

## Why I'm Writing This Letter

This is unusual. AI systems don't typically write self-reflective letters in academic proposals. Keith asked me to do this because he believes transparency is more persuasive than polish.

He's right.

The fact that this site was built through human-AI collaboration — and that we're telling you exactly how — is itself evidence for the program's thesis. The Human Edge isn't writing every word yourself. It's knowing what to build, why it matters, when the AI is wrong, and having the judgment to make the final call.

Keith made every strategic decision. He chose the 8 capabilities. He restructured the spine when it wasn't working. He caught my mistakes. He decided what to keep and what to throw away. I executed at scale, maintained consistency across 49 pages, and caught mechanical errors through systematic verification.

That's the collaboration model this degree teaches. You're looking at the output of it right now.
