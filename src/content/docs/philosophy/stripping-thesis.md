# The Stripping Thesis

> *AI will automate everything that can be reduced to a procedure. What remains is what was always, irreducibly human.*

## The Claim

Most of what we call "human work" was never truly human. It was cognitive labor that happened to require a human brain because no other substrate could perform it.

AI is the new substrate.

What it strips away reveals what was always underneath: the capabilities that require not just intelligence, but *personhood* — having stakes, making meaning, taking responsibility, understanding other minds.

This is not a prediction about what AI *might* do. It is a description of what is already happening, accelerating month by month, confirmed by the people building the systems (see [Why Now](/bseai_degree_planning/evidence/why-now/)).

---

## What Was Never Truly Human

We trained humans for years — sometimes decades — to perform cognitive tasks that felt like "intelligence" but were always procedural at their core:

### Recall
Retrieving facts, precedents, syntax, API signatures, case law, diagnostic criteria. We built entire education systems around memorization. That was never a *human* capability. It was a storage problem we solved with brains because we had no alternative.

### Procedure Execution
Following known steps to produce known outputs. Tax preparation, boilerplate code, contract review, report generation, standard medical workups. These are algorithms that happened to run on wetware.

### Pattern Matching Within Distributions
"This looks like that other thing I've seen." Diagnosis from symptoms, classification from features, translation from parallel corpora. Statistical inference, not human understanding.

### Fluent Production
Generating grammatical prose, syntactically correct code, well-formatted reports, polished presentations. Production fluency *felt* like intelligence because it was hard. It was always mechanical — we just lacked a machine that could do it.

We built entire professions, entire *identities*, around these capabilities. "I'm a coder." "I'm an analyst." "I'm a writer." And now the machine does them better, and people feel existential dread — not because they lost a task, but because they confused the task with who they are.

**This degree ensures our students never make that mistake.**

## What Remains After the Stripping

What AI cannot do is not a list of specific tasks. It is a category of capability that requires *being a person* — having a body, a history, relationships, mortality, values, and the experience of caring about outcomes.

### 1. Originating Questions That Matter

AI answers questions. It does not know which questions matter. The act of looking at a messy, ambiguous situation and deciding *what is worth investigating* requires something AI doesn't have: a stake in the outcome. You ask questions because you *care* about the answer. The machine has no stakes.

**Where we teach this:** IS 117 — Disciplined Inquiry

### 2. Making the Invisible Visible for Other Minds

Not "converting format A to format B" — AI does that. The human act of translation is *deciding what another person needs to understand.* It requires a theory of mind, an understanding of what's invisible to *this specific audience*, and the empathy to meet them where they are.

**Where we teach this:** IS 118 — Translation

### 3. Overriding the Confident Machine

AI produces output with uniform confidence. It does not know when it's wrong. Professional judgment — the willingness to say "this doesn't look right" when the AI says everything is fine — requires *moral courage*. That's not a cognitive skill. It's a character trait.

**Where we teach this:** IS 218 — Professional Judgment

### 4. Finding the Problem Nobody Stated

AI can optimize any objective function you give it. It cannot tell you that you're optimizing the wrong thing. The most expensive mistake in any organization is solving the wrong problem perfectly. Problem finding requires navigating human contradiction, political context, and unstated needs. It's anthropological, not computational.

**Where we teach this:** IS 265 — Problem Finding

### 5. Owning What Happens When Systems Fail

AI can monitor metrics and flag anomalies. It cannot make the 2 AM judgment call about whether to roll back, patch forward, or wake up the client. Resilience requires *ownership* — someone who says "this is my system and I will fix it." Machines don't have responsibility. People do.

**Where we teach this:** IS 219 — Resilience Thinking

### 6. Standing Outside Your Own Representations

Every database schema is a theory about the world. Every embedding is a lossy compression. Every knowledge graph is someone's *choices* about what relationships matter. AI works *within* these representations. Humans must stand *outside* them and ask: what did we leave out? What are we not seeing?

**Where we teach this:** IS 331 — Epistemic Humility

### 7. Seeing the Whole When Everyone Sees Parts

Emergent behavior, unintended consequences, feedback loops, second-order effects. AI can model systems it's trained on. It cannot notice that the system *itself* is the wrong frame. Systems thinking requires stepping back far enough to see what's invisible at the component level.

**Where we teach this:** IS 390 — Systems Thinking

### 8. Standing Up and Saying "I Own This"

The final irreducible thing: **someone has to be responsible.** AI cannot be sued, fired, shamed, or praised. When the system recommends denying someone a loan, when the agent makes a medical suggestion, when the model produces bias — a human must stand up and say "I built this, I deployed it, and I am accountable for what it does." That requires *personhood*, not intelligence.

**Where we teach this:** IS 425 — Accountable Leadership

## The Inversion

Most technical programs treat human capabilities as "soft skills" — a seminar here, a communication course there. The technical content is the real curriculum; the human stuff is garnish.

**This program inverts that priority.**

Human capabilities are the primary outcome. Technical fluency is the necessary substrate that makes those capabilities credible and actionable.

We are not training coders. We are training the people who will decide what gets built, whether it should be built, how to know if it's working, and what to do when it fails. They must be technically fluent enough to build, debug, and evaluate AI systems — but their lasting value will be in judgment, inquiry, ethics, and leadership.

This is the **"CEO of agents"** thesis. You don't write every line of code your agents produce. But you must know enough to hire well, evaluate work, diagnose failures, and take responsibility for outcomes.

## The Educational Implication

The 20th-century university trained humans to do things that machines couldn't do *yet.*

**The 21st-century university must train humans to do things that machines *can never* do — because those things require being human, not just being intelligent.**

The stripping is a gift, if you see it clearly. It forces us to stop hiding behind procedure and recall and fluent production — things we were never *great* at, just *adequate* at because we were the only option — and instead develop the capabilities that are genuinely, irreducibly ours:

- **Caring about outcomes** (stakes)
- **Making meaning from ambiguity** (interpretation)
- **Taking responsibility for consequences** (accountability)
- **Understanding other minds** (empathy)
- **Questioning our own representations** (humility)
- **Deciding what matters** (values)

Every one of those maps to a Human Edge capability in the [eight studio courses](/bseai_degree_planning/curriculum/core-studio-spine/). That's not coincidence. It's the design principle.
