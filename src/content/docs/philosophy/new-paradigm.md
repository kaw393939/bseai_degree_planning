# The New Paradigm

> *The old paradigm: invest 4–6 years learning a skill, monetize it for 20–30 years. That paradigm is over. The new paradigm: develop permanently human capabilities, apply them through whatever tools exist now, re-tool continuously. The human capabilities persist. The tools are disposable.*

---

## The Old Paradigm

For most of the 20th century, the career model was simple and reliable:

```
Invest 4–6 years learning a skill
       ↓
Monetize that skill for 20–30 years
       ↓
Retire on the accumulated value
```

This worked because skills had **long half-lives.** Learn COBOL in 1970, write COBOL until 2000. Learn surgery techniques in medical school, practice them for a career. Learn accounting rules, apply them for decades. The investment was high but the payoff was long.

Universities were optimized for this model: four years of intensive skill acquisition that yielded decades of professional return. Accreditation, curriculum design, degree structure — all of it assumed that what you learned would remain valuable long enough to justify the investment.

---

## Why the Old Paradigm Is Dead

AI collapses the half-life of *procedural* skills to months.

Matt Shumer, AI startup founder and investor, documented this in real time in February 2026:

> "A couple of months ago, I was going back and forth with the AI, guiding it, making edits. Now I just describe the outcome and leave."

His orchestration skill from November 2025 was automated by February 2026. **Three months.**

This isn't unique to programming. The same compression is happening across every knowledge profession:

| Profession | Old Half-Life | New Half-Life |
|---|---|---|
| Software developer (specific frameworks) | 3–5 years | 3–6 months |
| Financial analyst (modeling techniques) | 5–10 years | 6–12 months |
| Legal research | 10–15 years | 1–2 years |
| Medical diagnostics (pattern recognition) | 15–20 years | 2–3 years |
| Technical writing | 5–10 years | Already automated |

When the half-life of a skill is shorter than the time required to learn it, **the invest-then-monetize model collapses.** You cannot justify four years of education to acquire skills that will be automated before graduation.

Unless you're teaching something different.

## The New Paradigm

```
Develop judgment + adaptability + human capabilities
       ↓
Apply them through whatever tools exist NOW
       ↓
Re-tool continuously as the substrate changes
       ↓
The human capabilities persist; the tools are disposable
```

The critical insight: **some capabilities have infinite half-lives** because they are not procedural — they are constitutive of being human:

| Capability | Half-Life | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Asking the right question | ∞ | Requires stakes, not just intelligence |
| Exercising judgment under uncertainty | ∞ | Requires courage, not just analysis |
| Taking responsibility for outcomes | ∞ | Requires personhood, not just competence |
| Understanding what other people need | ∞ | Requires empathy, not just data |
| Seeing the whole system | ∞ | Requires perspective, not just processing |
| Deciding what matters | ∞ | Requires values, not just optimization |

These are exactly the [eight Human Edge capabilities](/bseai_degree_planning/curriculum/outcomes/) this degree develops.

## The Studio Model as Paradigm Response

The BS in Enterprise AI doesn't just *teach differently*. It's built on a fundamentally different model of what education is for.

### Old Model: Knowledge Transfer
- Professor has knowledge → transfers it to student → student stores it → student applies it for 30 years
- Assessment: "Can you recall and apply what I taught you?"
- Value proposition: "You'll know things the market needs"

### New Model: Capability Development
- Student encounters real problems → develops human capabilities through building → capabilities persist regardless of tools
- Assessment: "Can you exercise judgment, ask the right questions, and take responsibility?"
- Value proposition: "You'll be the person the tools can't replace"

### How the Studio Spine Implements This

Every semester, students enter a new studio with:
- A **new technical domain** (the tools change every semester — by design)
- A **new Human Edge capability** to develop (these accumulate permanently)
- A **new Context Pack** to construct (transferring meaning to agents — the meta-skill of the AI era)

The technical content in IS 117 (HTML, CSS, Git) will be partially obsolete by the time those students take IS 331 (databases, knowledge graphs). **That's not a bug — it's the point.** The Disciplined Inquiry capability they developed is permanent. The specific HTML/CSS workflow was just the vehicle.

By graduation, students have been through **eight cycles of "the ground shifted, now adapt."** That experience — repeated under increasing complexity — is what makes perpetual adaptation feel normal, not terrifying.

## The 40/60 Split: Operationalizing the Paradigm

Every studio course in the spine follows a **40/60 rule:**

- **40% hard-way** — Students do foundational work manually to build understanding of *what the machine is doing*. You can't exercise judgment over AI output if you don't understand the domain.
- **60% agentic** — Students direct AI agents to build real systems, practicing the actual workflow they'll use for the rest of their careers: constructing context, evaluating output, iterating, and taking responsibility.

This isn't a compromise between old and new pedagogy. It's the precise ratio that produces **informed judgment** — the defining capability of the AI era.

**Why 40%, not less?** Without procedural understanding, you can't judge AI output. A student who has never written a SQL query can't evaluate whether an AI-generated query is correct, efficient, or dangerous. The 40% builds the substrate for judgment.

**Why 60%, not more?** Because the 40% hard-way establishes credibility quickly. Once students understand the domain well enough to evaluate AI, the remaining time should develop the workflow they'll actually use: directing agents, managing context, evaluating output, iterating. That's the job.

## The Context Pack: The Meta-Skill

In the old paradigm, the critical meta-skill was *studying* — absorbing and retaining information.

In the new paradigm, the critical meta-skill is **context management** — assembling and transferring the right information to AI agents so they can work effectively on your behalf.

The **Context Pack** is this degree's recurring artifact. Every studio, students build one:

| Component | What It Contains | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Project Brief** | Problem statement, constraints, success criteria | Agents need clear objectives, not vague instructions |
| **Domain Context** | Key concepts, terminology, relationships, prior work | Agents need domain knowledge to produce relevant output |
| **Evaluation Criteria** | How to judge whether the output is good | Without this, you can't exercise judgment |
| **Prior Context** | What's been tried, what worked, what failed | Prevents the agent from repeating known mistakes |

As students progress through the spine, their Context Packs become more sophisticated:

- **IS 117:** Simple context pack for a static website project
- **IS 218:** Context pack for a full-stack app with testing requirements
- **IS 265:** Context pack that includes stakeholder analysis and organizational politics
- **IS 331:** Context pack that includes data assumptions and epistemic limitations
- **IS 425:** Context pack for a production AI system with knowledge graphs, evaluation harnesses, and HITL requirements

By graduation, constructing a Context Pack for *any* domain is second nature. That's the operational skill behind "CEO of agents."

## The Investment Case

For students and parents: This degree doesn't teach skills with a 6-month half-life. It develops capabilities with an infinite half-life, delivered through a studio model that also produces genuine technical fluency and a portfolio of shipped work.

The [job market evidence](/bseai_degree_planning/evidence/job-market/) shows that employers are already paying $137,000–$350,000 for exactly the combination this degree produces: AI tool fluency + professional judgment + cross-functional collaboration + documentation + evaluation + accountability.

For administrators: This isn't a speculative bet on one technology. It's a structural response to a pattern that has occurred with every general-purpose technology in history. The question isn't whether the investment model will change. It's whether your institution adapts before or after the change is obvious.

### The Productivity Imperative

The investment case extends beyond individual career returns. The U.S. faces $38.5T in gross federal debt, with ~$1T/yr in interest costs climbing to an estimated $2.1T by 2036 (CBO, Feb 2026). The only non-regressive exit is productivity growth — and AI is the only tool capable of delivering at scale. Goldman Sachs projects +1.4 pp/yr for a decade; McKinsey projects +0.5–3.4 pp/yr including automation.

But every projection includes a conditional: gains require **skilled human deployment**, not just better models. The four largest hyperscalers are deploying ~$600B+ in AI infrastructure in 2026 alone (CNBC, Barron's). The federal government has responded with a dedicated White House AI Education Task Force, 90+ actions in the "Winning the AI Race" plan, and an Executive Order on Advancing AI Education (Apr 2025, whitehouse.gov).

The missing piece is people. Not model builders — too few needed, and models improve themselves. Not prompt engineers — the skill is already being automated. The missing piece is **professionals who can deploy AI into complex organizations, evaluate whether it works, and take accountability for the result.** That's what this degree produces.
